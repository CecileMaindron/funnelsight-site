Cheat sheet: 10 funnel metrics to track first, and when to read them

Teams often end up in one of two places. Either nobody is sure what to track, or the dashboard holds forty metrics in no particular order. In both cases, nobody knows which number to look at on Monday, or when a change can be judged.

This cheat sheet sorts ten funnel metrics by when they become readable, who owns each one and what it's used for. It works for a new product, and just as well for a new channel, a new audience or a new market.

The cheat sheet

How to read it

The three blocks follow the time each metric needs. Sign-ups by source mean something on day one. Trial-to-paid needs at least one full trial. Churn and payback need a quarter of deals.

"Used for" separates four needs. Daily work is what a team acts on during the week. Tests tell you whether a change worked. Reporting goes into a quarterly review or a leadership update. Forecast and budget is what finance plans with. A metric can serve several of them, but no one needs all ten at the same pace. Leadership doesn't need sign-ups per day, and a growth team can't steer its week on net revenue retention.

Activation and PQL are easy to confuse. Activation means a new user has done once what they came for. A PQL means their usage shows they're more likely to buy than the average user. Time to value comes from the same event as activation: the number of days it took to get there.

What to set up before you can read anything

Most of these numbers are already collected somewhere, so there's no need to start from scratch. Traffic and on-page conversion are in Google Analytics and Search Console. Sign-ups, activation and PQLs are in the product's own data. MQLs, nurturing and lead-to-opportunity are in the marketing automation tool and the CRM. Trial-to-paid, churn and revenue are in billing or the CRM. What's usually missing is the link between them, and a few written rules.

You can't judge a channel before it brings paying customers. But if its sources aren't recorded the same way from the first day, you won't be able to judge it when they come. Write down which touch counts and how each source is named, and the date of each change. Do the same for the activation event.

Expect the first months of a channel to cost more than they bring back while it ramps up. A payback read after three weeks says more about the timing than about the channel.

How long before a change shows up

Every change has its own delay. Launch paid search on Monday, and sign-ups from it show the same day. Whether those users activate shows within a few days. Send a webinar invitation and most registrations arrive within 48 hours. Rewrite an SEO page and you may wait weeks.

Three things change the delay. The first is where the change happens. A new prompt or offer during the trial reaches trials that are already running, so trial-to-paid moves within days. A change before sign-up only reaches people who sign up after it, so you wait one full trial. Even then, users who are already convinced often keep their 14 free days before they pay.

The second is volume. A new third email in a nurturing sequence, or a new step in a sales sequence, only shows once enough people have reached it.

The third is the window you compare. A change made on the 25th barely shows in that month's figures, and one week of effect gets lost in a quarter. Start the comparison on the day of the change, and compare the people who came after it with those who came just before.

Three traps when you build the dashboard

  • Alerts on the dashboard. Sign-ups per day, traffic without a source or a broken sync are there to catch a bug. Set an alert for them and keep them off the dashboard.
  • A strategic metric read every week. Net revenue retention moves with renewals. Read weekly, it mostly shows noise.
  • One average for every source. A blended activation rate can stay flat while one source improves and another drops. Read the first six metrics by source.

What the dashboard needs to show

To follow this order without rebuilding the numbers each month, a dashboard needs four things. Each volume metric next to the one that completes it, such as sign-ups next to cost per paying customer. Each channel read on its own timeline, paid search over days and SEO over months. A dated note on the day of each change. And one definition of each metric, used in every view, as in a single source of truth.

What are the most important SaaS metrics?

Finance teams usually start with recurring revenue, churn and net revenue retention. For marketing and growth teams, the useful starting set sits earlier in the funnel: sign-ups by source, activation, PQL or lead-to-opportunity rate, and trial-to-paid. Revenue metrics follow once enough deals are in.

What are funnel metrics?

Funnel metrics measure how people move from one stage of the journey to the next, such as visit to sign-up or trial to paid, and how long that takes. Read by source, they show which channels bring people who go further, not just more people.
