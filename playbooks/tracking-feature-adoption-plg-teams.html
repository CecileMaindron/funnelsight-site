Feature adoption rate: how to calculate it, and what it means for revenue

Feature adoption rate is the share of users, or accounts, who use a given feature over a period. It answers a question many SaaS companies can't: which features do customers actually use, and which ones made them buy or stay?

The usual report ranks features by the share of all users who touched them. It's the easiest number to pull, and it misleads in two ways. A paid add-on looks like a failure because most customers never bought it, and the feature everyone opens isn't always the one people pay for.

How to calculate feature adoption rate

Formula

Feature adoption rate (%) = accounts that used the feature in the period ÷ accounts that could use it × 100

Replace accounts with users for a user-level rate. Track both if you can: the account rate says how many customers rely on the feature, the user rate how far it has spread inside them.

Before you calculate it, decide three things.

  • Which accounts to count. Count only the accounts that have access to the feature: those whose plan includes it, or that bought the add-on. If you count every account, a shift between plans will look like a change in adoption.
  • What counts as use. Opening a feature once means someone tried it. Whether they adopted it depends on how often the feature is meant to be used. A monthly report used every month is adopted. A daily dashboard opened once is not. Track tried and adopted separately.
  • Who to exclude. Remove your own team and your test accounts. They are often the heaviest users of a new feature.

Say 40 of your 1,600 customers bought a reporting add-on, and 22 of them use it every month. Against all customers, adoption is 1.4%. Against the accounts that bought it, adoption is 55%. That's the figure to track, and it raises a second question: 18 accounts pay for something they don't use, and each one is a renewal at risk.

Where feature adoption fits in the customer lifecycle

The rate is calculated the same way at every stage. What changes is who you count, and who acts on the answer.

In the first two stages, adoption shows which features attract people and which ones convince them to buy. That shapes your campaigns and what goes into the trial. Usage alone can be misleading here. In this illustrative example, the feature almost every trial user runs does little to make them pay.

In the last three stages, the question is whether customers use what they bought. A paid module nobody uses is an early sign that the renewal is at risk. Customers who use features from a higher plan, during a preview for example, show where an upgrade conversation can start. It's one of several expansion signals, and the one feature adoption measures directly.

Which feature requests are worth building?

Requests reach the product team through sales, support, customer success and surveys. Counting them favours the customers who speak loudest. Following the biggest customer favours a single contract. Neither says how many accounts would use the result.

Weigh each request by the accounts behind it: how many there are, how much revenue they represent, and whether similar accounts that never asked would use it too. Before writing a spec, ask what the customer is trying to get done. As Marty Cagan argues, customers are a better source of problems than of solutions. A request for a spreadsheet export often points to a report that's missing.

Adoption then checks the decision. A few weeks after release, do the accounts that asked for the feature use it? Tracked from one release to the next, that rate shows how much a request is worth in your product. If requested features are rarely adopted, requests describe problems well and solutions badly, and the team should dig further before building.

The same data helps decide what to stop. Intercom's feature audit plots each feature by how many customers use it and how often. A feature few customers use, and rarely, can be improved, brought to the customers who need it, or retired. Each one kept adds maintenance, support and onboarding work.

What a useful feature adoption report shows

Each of these decisions needs usage read next to something a product analytics tool doesn't hold.

  • By account, with what it pays. The plan, the add-ons bought, the revenue and the renewal date, so an unused module shows up well before the renewal.
  • Next to what customers asked for and said. Feature requests and survey answers, such as NPS, attached to the account. A score is easy to count, but the comment and the account's usage explain it.
  • Before and after each release. A dated note on the release, and the accounts that asked for the feature, with their use 30 and 60 days later.
  • By acquisition source. Marketing then sees whether the features its campaigns promise are the ones new customers use.

What's the difference between feature adoption and product adoption?

Product adoption rate looks at the product as a whole: the share of new users or customers who become regular users. Feature adoption rate looks at one feature, among the users or accounts that can use it. A product can be well adopted while some of its features are barely used, which is what a feature audit brings out.
