Sales and marketing alignment starts with how the target is split

Updated November 26, 2026

The field marketer for South EMEA meets the region's sales director to review the quarter. Marketing opens with good news: leads are on track for the region, and the year looks good. The sales director agrees on the total, then adds: "My mid-market team is behind on quota. What are you going to do about it?" Both are reading the right numbers. The marketer reads the region, by channel. The sales director carries a quota for each team.

Why sales and marketing still aren't aligned with a shared target

The usual causes are well documented: each team measured on different numbers, two definitions of a qualified lead, slow handoffs, separate tools. The gap is also wider than leaders tend to think. In Forrester's 2024 surveys, 82% of C-level B2B professionals said their product, sales and marketing teams were aligned, while 65% of sales and marketing professionals saw a lack of alignment.

Some teams have fixed the visible part: one CRM, written definitions, an MQL target both sides signed. What remains is the way that target reaches each team.

Finance sets a revenue target for new business. The sales leadership splits it into quotas, by region, then by account size, industry or country, and each team knows its number. Marketing receives its share as a total for each region, since that is how its budgets are set, and works back to an MQL target with average conversion rates (the pipeline planning page shows that calculation).

Along the way, the target loses the shape sales carries. Whether marketing is measured on MQLs, SQLs or pipeline, each MQL or dollar counts the same toward the region's total, whatever team it goes to. For a mid-market account executive, a lead from a 20-person company counts for nothing.

When sales says the leads are bad, where to look first

"Bad leads" covers several problems. Asking sales what made each rejected lead bad is the quickest way to tell them apart, and the answers usually fall into a few groups: fit (company size, industry, geography), timing, contact data, or a lead that reached the wrong rep.

Look at fit first, since it is the problem a regional total hides. In the review above, both sides read the numbers by region and by channel, and the split sales uses for its quotas appears in neither. Put what marketing delivered to each sales team next to what that team needed. When one team is short while another has more than it can work, the complaint is about where the leads land. Better lead scoring won't move them.

Which team falls behind depends on the channels. Search, content and free trials bring whoever is looking for an answer, often smaller companies: you pick the keywords, not the size of the company behind the search. Account-based programs target a list of the largest accounts. The middle of the market gets neither, unless someone plans for it. The same happens by industry, or by country when a region's figures mostly reflect its largest market.

If the split is right and the complaint stays, move to the next checks: how fast leads were contacted, whether they were routed to a rep who covers their segment, and whether their contact data was usable.

We're on track for leads. Why is sales still short?

A quota missed today mostly comes from leads delivered in earlier quarters, all the more where sales cycles are long. What marketing delivers this quarter shows up in the quotas of the next ones. The table won't rescue the current quarter, but it shows whether the next one will start with the same gap, while there is still time to move budget. The same check works with pipeline instead of MQLs, if that is what marketing's target is set on.

How to set marketing pipeline targets that match sales quotas

The usual calculation still applies: start from the revenue target, divide by the average deal size and the win rate to get the opportunities needed, apply marketing's share, then work back to SQLs and MQLs with your conversion rates. What changes is where you run it: once per sales team instead of once per region, with each team's own deal size, win rate and conversion rates. The pipeline coverage each team needs follows from its win rate.

Deal sizes differ from one team to another, so the same revenue takes a different number of MQLs in each. With a target per team, the definition of a qualified lead includes the team's criteria. A lead from a company outside a team's range doesn't count toward that team's target, which turns "lead quality" into something both sides can measure.

Agree before the quarter starts on what happens when one team is ahead and another behind: which budget moves, from which channel, and who decides. Account-based campaigns and field events take longer to produce leads, so the check has to come early enough for the money moved to make a difference.

Some leads will still land outside the plan. Decide where they go: a lead from a 20-person company in a mid-market territory can be routed to the SMB team instead of being rejected, and counted for that team.

Pipeline reporting both teams can trust

MQL volume shows whether marketing delivered. The MQL to SQL rate shows whether those MQLs fit what sales can work, and depends on both teams. Pipeline sourced or influenced by marketing, and the win rate on those deals, show whether the definition of a qualified lead holds: if MQLs keep becoming SQLs but rarely turn into won deals, the definition needs work. These figures come from the CRM, and each one carries the rule behind it: which touches count, over what window, and how credit is shared. "Sourced", "touched" and "influenced" mean different things from one company to another, so the rule matters more than the label.

Poor leads or poor follow-up: how do you tell?

Compare leads contacted quickly with leads contacted late, within the same segment. If leads contacted within a day still don't turn into opportunities, the problem is more likely fit or timing. If conversion drops as the delay grows, follow-up is the place to work. A required rejection reason in the CRM gives the rest of the answer.

What is a sales and marketing SLA?

A service level agreement sets what each team commits to. Marketing commits to a volume of leads that meet the agreed definition, per sales team. Sales commits to a response time, and to a reason for every lead it rejects. It works when both sides review it with the same report.

What is a single source of truth for sales and marketing?

One set of definitions both teams apply to the same CRM records: what counts as a lead, an MQL, an opportunity, a sourced deal. It doesn't mean a single number. Sales and marketing can report different figures from the same data, as long as each figure states the rule behind it.

Does attribution prove a channel caused a deal?

No. An attribution model shows which touches came before a deal. It can't tell whether the deal would have happened without one of them. It's a way to compare channels, not proof of cause.
