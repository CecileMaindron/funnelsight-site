The hidden cost of chasing signup volume

Shortening the sign-up form or opening a new channel raises the sign-up count, often at a lower cost. What it brings in customers shows up further down the funnel, where few reports look.

A shorter form, one quarter later

A SaaS team with a free trial cuts its sign-up form from six fields to three, removing phone number, company and job title. The acquisition budget, paid and content together, stays at $75k a quarter. Sign-ups go up, and the quarterly report calls it a win. To check whether the win holds, the team follows those sign-ups in Funnelsight, all the way to the customers sales signed.

A shorter form lowers the bar to entry, so the extra sign-ups include more people who just want to look. And without a phone number, reps reached fewer PQLs.

What chasing sign-ups does to the rest of the funnel

A target set on sign-ups alone tends to start a chain of reactions, each easy to justify on its own.

More sign-ups look like the answer. A shorter form, a new channel or a new profile, such as another company size, vertical or job title, can all raise volume, often at a lower cost per sign-up. Testing them is how a team finds room to grow, but it's unknown territory, and 10% more sign-ups at the top rarely means 10% more customers at the bottom.

Then the PQL bar gets the blame. When PQLs don't keep pace with sign-ups, it's tempting to conclude the bar is too strict and lower it until they do. A PQL, like an MQL, shouldn't be redefined to follow pipeline swings. A conversion rate that stays far too high or too low is a reason to revisit the bar, while one weak quarter isn't.

Then lead quality gets the blame. When sales accepts fewer PQLs, the usual explanation is that the leads got worse. That only holds if the audience changed, since a new channel or profile can bring people who use the product the same way without being buyers. With the same audience and the same bar, a PQL is worth what it was before. The cause is more often what's missing around it, such as the profile reps rely on to route and follow up each lead.

Leads don't carry the pipeline alone

Behind these reactions is the idea that pipeline is lead generation's job. Even the best leads get lost if the product doesn't show its value during the trial, or if the follow-up from sales is slow or generic. Redefining the hot lead in a self-serve motion looks at when sales should step in, and what counts as a PQL at how to set the bar.

What to watch after each change

Each of these moves can be the right call. What matters is checking the step after the one it was meant to improve.

Does this mean the signup form should never change?

No. Testing fields can help. Change one field at a time, leave it long enough to read the result, and check its effect down to the leads sales accepts, not just the sign-up count.
