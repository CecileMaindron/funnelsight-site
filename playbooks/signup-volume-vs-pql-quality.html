The hidden cost of chasing signup volume

Two moments come up again and again in growth teams. Someone suggests shrinking the signup form to get more leads. Someone on sales asks why a lead needs to clear a PQL bar at all when pipeline is tight. Neither move is automatically wrong, and neither one is free.

Why a shorter form looks like the fix

A longer signup form is an easy target when the number everyone is watching is total signups. Fewer fields, less friction, more people through the door. Testing that idea isn't the problem: trying a shorter form, or swapping one field for another, can genuinely move the needle, and the usual discipline applies here like anywhere else. Change one field at a time, and give it enough time to read the result, instead of cycling the form every few weeks and never knowing which change actually did anything.

The part that gets skipped is checking what a removed field was doing further down the funnel, not just at the signup step. Country and phone number are the two that come up most. Drop country and compliance rules a team relies on elsewhere can quietly stop applying correctly. Drop phone number and email becomes the only way to reach that person later, which matters the day they go quiet: a dormant or churned trial user has no reason to hand over a phone number after the fact, so if email isn't working, there's no second channel left. A shorter form that moves the signup number up while usage, activation, or PQL volume moves down hasn't fixed anything. It's moved the same problem one step further down the funnel.

Why calling a signup five minutes in solves nothing

A rep reaching out to a trial user minutes after signup is usually trying to compensate for something else: a trial that isn't quite self-serve enough, or a worry that the prospect won't get set up correctly alone. Both are legitimate reasons to get involved, if the product genuinely needs it. What's harder to justify is reaching out as a default, on every signup, regardless of whether the person has done anything yet.

If a signup needs a salesperson that early, the question isn't really about timing. It's about the trial itself. What does a self-serve trial offer over asking the prospect to book a demo? And from the rep's side, someone who signed up five minutes ago and hasn't logged in yet usually doesn't have much to talk about. There's also a simpler signal worth taking seriously: someone who picked "start a trial" over "contact us" or "request a demo" chose that path for a reason. At that stage, they weren't looking for a conversation with sales. Our page on redefining the hot lead in a self-serve motion goes further into when sales should actually step in.

The same fight marketing already had over the MQL bar

Raise the PQL bar and sales complains there isn't enough volume to work with. Lower it and sales complains the leads aren't worth calling. That's not a new argument: marketing has been having some version of this fight over the MQL bar for years. The criteria behind a PQL can genuinely be too strict, or too loose, and worth revisiting on that basis. What doesn't hold up is adjusting them every time sales asks, without looking at what actually happens to conversion further down the funnel once the bar moves.

What to watch after each change

Each of these moves can be the right call. What matters is checking the step after the one it was meant to improve.

What to watch after each change
What you changeWhat it can improveWhat to watch next
A shorter sign-up formMore sign-upsActivation and PQLs per sign-up, and whether you can still reach the people who go quiet
A lower PQL barMore PQLs for salesHow the PQLs that only clear the new bar convert, and the time reps spend on each one
Calling new sign-ups soonerFaster contactWhether those trials activate and convert better, or simply get a call they didn't ask for
A higher PQL barBetter PQLsWhether sales still has enough to work on, and how many good accounts now buy without help

What this looks like in practice

A growth team lowers its PQL threshold for a quarter to keep sales fed, the kind of change that's easy to justify in the moment. Looking at the segmented view instead of a single pipeline total, they see the accounts that only cleared the new, lower threshold convert at a visibly lower rate than the ones that would have cleared the original bar, while the time reps spend per PQL goes up, not down. The threshold goes back up the next quarter, this time with the team watching segment-level conversion instead of just the raw PQL count. Does this mean the signup form should never change?

No. Testing and adjusting fields can genuinely help. The point is to change one field at a time and check the effect across the whole funnel, not just the signup count.

Should sales ever contact a trial user directly?

Yes, when the trial genuinely isn't self-serve enough for the prospect to get there alone. It shouldn't be the default reflex every time pipeline feels tight.
