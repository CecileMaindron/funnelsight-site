Expanding self-serve PLG to a new country

The decision to open a new market is made. What follows decides whether it works: which channels to start with, what to localize first, how self-serve and sales share the work, and how the central team and the local team read the same numbers.

Start from the demand you already have

A self-serve product that has been findable for a while usually has users in a market before anyone targets it. Those sign-ups are the starting point: how many come in each month, how they found the product, and whether they activate and convert at the same rate as everywhere else. A few hundred of them, even from a spreadsheet export, are enough for a first read. In Funnelsight, the country is one more filter on the funnel you already track, with the same activation event as the rest of the product, so a new market starts with a comparison rather than a blank page.

What shapes the channel mix

The channel mix that works at home rarely transfers as it is. Four things decide what it should look like in a new market.

What shapes the channel mix in a new market
What to look atWhat it changes
How mature the market isWhen buyers don't know the category yet, few of them search for it. The first channels are then the ones that explain it, such as content, events or partners.
How people buySome markets buy through integrators or resellers, others pay online, and in some the first conversation happens in a messaging app such as WhatsApp. The channels have to follow those habits.
Who is already thereA well-established local competitor has already shaped what buyers expect, from the price to whether they can try the product before talking to anyone.
How trust is builtIn some markets a self-serve trial is enough to buy. In others, a call or a meeting still decides, and the channel mix has to make room for it.

Once the first channels are chosen, each one has to be judged on its own clock. Paid search shows within days whether people respond, while a local guide or a series of articles takes months to build. A partner adds the length of their own sales cycle, and an event only pays off once the deals it started have closed. A single monthly report that puts them side by side favors the fastest channel by default. Set a decision date for each channel before the test starts, and judge it on what counts in that market: the accounts it brings that activate, convert, and pay back what the channel cost. Funnelsight shows that by source and by country, with each source's payback time next to its sign-up volume.

What to localize first, and what can wait

Plenty of self-serve products enter a new market without translating the site, changing the currency or adapting their prices, and do fine as long as the audience buys in English. What can't wait is what makes the trial legal and usable there, starting with the consent and data rules that let the team capture and follow up the leads it brings in.

What to localize first in a new market
Can't waitCan wait until demand is proven
  • Consent and data rules for capturing and following up trial leads
  • Tax registration where it's required
  • A consistent journey: an ad in the local language leads to a page in that language, and the rest of the path follows
  • A translated site, product and content
  • Local currency and local pricing
  • Support in local hours, as long as you say clearly when support is available

Two choices need more care when a sales team or partners are involved. Prices that differ from one country to the next invite customers to buy where it's cheaper, or to contest the price, so local pricing needs a clear rule before anyone quotes it. And the split between buying online and going through sales or a partner can differ by country. Where buyers expect a local contract, accounts tend to stall at the checkout: activation holds up, then conversion drops at the last step. That shows in the funnel for that country. The same usage signals that flag a product qualified lead can then send those accounts to sales or a partner as warm leads, with their usage attached, instead of leaving them at a checkout page.

Rules that don't translate

GDPR applies the moment a company touches EU user data, wherever that company is incorporated. It doesn't require data to stay in a given country, but some sectors add their own rules. In Germany, for example, health and social data processed in the cloud must stay in Germany, the EU, the EEA, Switzerland or a country with an EU adequacy decision. A product without the right hosting options can't promise that and sort it out later.

The UK breaks the "Europe is one market" assumption too. Since Brexit, UK VAT is a separate system: registering for EU VAT, including the EU's one-stop shop, doesn't cover UK sales. A business based outside the UK that sells digital services to UK consumers has to register from the first sale, with no threshold. Sales to UK businesses generally fall under the reverse charge, where the customer accounts for the VAT.

Public buyers in Germany can award contracts to companies based anywhere, but tenders are mostly published and run in German, through dedicated procurement platforms, and federal bodies require electronic invoices in a set format. A local reseller or partner is often the practical way in, even for a product the company would rather sell directly.

One set of definitions, local views

Expansion usually means working with people who don't report to the central team: a country manager, a partner, a local agency. Many disagreements start with the numbers. The local team counts a lead one way, headquarters another, and the first meetings go into reconciling them. Sharing the definitions moves the discussion to decisions. The activation event, the attribution rule and the funnel stages are set once for every market. Each team builds the view it needs on those numbers, and local context, such as a public holiday, a local campaign or a payment outage, goes into a dated note on that period. A dip in one country is then explained before it's debated.

A first quarter in a new market

  1. Set the starting point (weeks 1 to 3)

    Pull the existing sign-ups, activation and conversion for that country. Run a data check to see what's missing, such as campaign tags or deals with no source, and agree on the definitions with the local team.

  2. Test a few channels (weeks 3 to 8)

    Pick two or three channels based on what the market looks like, each with a budget and a decision date. In self-serve, make sure the journey is consistent and the checkout works before paying for traffic.

  3. Decide what comes next (weeks 8 to 12)

    Compare each channel and the country's funnel with the starting point. Then decide what to scale, what to localize next, and where accounts should go to sales or a partner rather than a checkout page.

Does this mean translation never matters?

No. Translation earns its place once demand is confirmed and the journey can be localized from start to finish: the ad, the page it leads to, the product and the follow-up. A journey that switches language halfway can do worse than a consistent one in English.

Should I expect the same conversion rate in every market?

No, and that's not a red flag by itself. Payment habits, buying culture and plain regulatory friction move the number up or down without the product being at fault. Compare a new market to its own numbers over time. Forcing it to match an unrelated baseline on day one just produces a bad decision dressed up as data.
