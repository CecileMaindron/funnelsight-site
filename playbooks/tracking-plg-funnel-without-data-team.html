Track your PLG funnel without a data team

Funnel tracking often waits until an analyst or an engineer is free. It doesn't have to: a first useful view needs a few exports and one agreed definition, and it can be in place the same day.

Why funnel tracking usually waits on a data team

Activation and retention data tends to live in two places: the product itself, and the CRM. Pulling both into one view by hand, in a spreadsheet, works for a while. Then the team grows, the number of signals grows with it, and the spreadsheet stops being something anyone trusts. At that point, the instinct is to wait for a data hire or an engineering sprint before doing anything more structured. That wait can stretch for months, and in the meantime nobody has a clear read on where users actually drop off.

What a first view should answer

A first view doesn't need twenty metrics. It needs to answer three questions well:

  • Of the people who signed up last month, how many reached the activation event, and how long did it take them?
  • Which sources bring the sign-ups that activate, not just the most sign-ups?
  • Do the users who activated still use the product a few weeks later?

A view that answers those three is already more useful than a dashboard full of numbers nobody acts on.

The last line is the only one you can't export. It's also the one most likely to cause arguments later, so it's worth settling first: what counts as activation in PLG walks through how to choose it.

No product analytics tool yet?

Not every team has one, and it's tempting to wait until it does. A product analytics tool isn't a prerequisite, though, and it comes with its own work: someone has to instrument the product so it sends clean events, and in Europe users usually have to consent to being tracked. In the meantime, other signals can stand in for usage:

  • The product's own database, which already records what accounts create, connect or configure.
  • Light tracking on the web app, when the product runs online.
  • Requests that can only come from inside the product, such as a contact form or a feature that only shows up after a few days of use. When one arrives, it confirms the user got that far.
  • A thank-you page after a download. A visit confirms the download started, and a short form on that page can tell you who it was.

Starting this way has another benefit. Laying out the funnel step by step, with the data you actually have, quickly shows where the gaps are and which events a product analytics tool would need to capture. Choosing that tool, and briefing whoever implements it, is easier once you know what the first view couldn't answer. In Funnelsight, the data check lists what it can and can't see, so those gaps are written down from the first import.

How to start without one

Funnelsight is built so a marketing or growth person can set up a working view alone. You import a spreadsheet to get started and test the setup before connecting a live product data source, so there's nothing to provision on day one. Once you've settled on the action, or short sequence of actions, that shows up consistently among the users who stick around, you define that event once. It is the same mechanism behind Funnelsight's activation and retention dashboard. A working view can be in place the same day, without a dedicated analytics tool or a data engineer involved.

Starting simple doesn't mean using unreliable data. It means limiting scope at first, not cutting corners on rigor. In practice, cover one persona or segment before all of them, and set your data refresh on a schedule, hourly or daily, rather than aiming for a continuous stream you don't need yet. Both are configurable later, once you know what you're actually looking for.

What a first pass looks like

A growth marketer exports the current user list from the CRM, with each user's acquisition source, and a usage export from the product. They import both into Funnelsight as a spreadsheet and define "created first project" as the activation event. Within the same session, they can see how many signed-up users reached that event, which sources those users came from, and how that group's retention compares over the following weeks. There's no dashboard to build from scratch and no query to write. Once the setup earns its keep, the next step is connecting a live source, so the view updates on its own schedule instead of through a manual re-import.

Do I need engineering help to set this up?

No. Importing a spreadsheet is enough to get started, and a working dashboard can be in place the same day.

Can I start with one metric before covering everything?

Yes. Covering a single activation event or one segment first, then expanding, is a reasonable way to start: it keeps the data rigorous while limiting what you take on at once.
