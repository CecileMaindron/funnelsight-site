When activation drops: what time to value tells you

The activation rate fell from 34% to 29% this month. Before anyone changes the onboarding or the campaign mix, a second number shows where to look: how long the users who did activate took to get there.

In the first case, the users who activate do it as fast as before, so the product works as it did and the change is in who signs up. In the second, the users who activate take twice as long, which points to something in the product that got harder.

Two measures, one stage

The activation rate is the share of sign-ups that reach the activation event within the trial. Time to value is the number of days between sign-up and that same event, so two dates per user are enough to calculate it. The moment someone understands why the product is worth using can't be timestamped, and the activation event is the measurable action that shows they got there.

Read both per weekly or monthly cohort, over several periods. A shift that shows in one cohort is often noise, while a shift that holds over several cohorts is a trend.

Where the problem starts

What each cause calls for

An audience change starts with the channel or campaign owner, using the segment data rather than an assumption. The cause can sit in the promise itself, since a campaign that promises something the product doesn't do brings sign-ups no onboarding will win back. A product change starts with the step where users stall, and the wording on that step is often the cheapest thing to test before rebuilding a flow. Users who left before activating need something to bring them back, such as an email pointing to the exact step where they stopped. For accounts worth a conversation, an offer of help from a person can do what an email can't.

Once the cause is clear, a dated note on that period keeps the explanation next to the number, so the same dip isn't debated again at the next review. None of this works if the activation event itself changed in the meantime, since a new definition can look exactly like a drop.

What's a good time to value?

There's no universal benchmark worth quoting, since it depends on the product and on the activation event chosen. A product set up in minutes and one that needs a data connection can't be compared. What matters is your own trend, cohort after cohort.

Should the trial last as long as time to value?

It should at least leave room for it: if most users need eight days to activate, a seven-day trial ends too soon. A much longer trial has its own cost, though, since it delays payment and can lose people who were ready to buy. Letting active users extend their trial, or letting early buyers start their paid plan with billing deferred to the trial's end, covers both cases.
