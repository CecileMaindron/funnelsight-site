Time to value: why it matters in a free trial

Updated November 26, 2026

Time to value (TTV) is how long a new user takes to reach a first meaningful result in the product, often called the aha moment. In a free trial, it tells you whether users get there early enough to judge the product, and decide to buy, before the trial ends.

Why time to value matters in a free trial

The first value should come early, ideally in the first half of the trial. Users who get there quickly have seen the product work for them, and they still have time to be convinced.

That time matters because a trial is where users decide whether to buy, on their own or with sales. The sooner they reach value, the more of the trial is left to test it on real work and, when needed, bring colleagues in or get the purchase approved.

Activating early doesn't make a user buy. The users who get there first may simply have had an easier start: the access they needed, more technical skills, or a precise use in mind. A faster onboarding won't turn every slow user into a customer, but it gives more of them the time to decide.

How to measure time to value

Time to value runs from sign-up to the moment the user first sees what the product does for them, often called the aha moment. That moment can't be recorded as such, so teams use the activation event as the marker: the first time the product does its job for the user, such as a first report built from connected data. Measured this way, it is also called time to first value (TTFV).

Formula

Time to value (days) = activation date − sign-up date

Read it per weekly or monthly cohort. Rather than an average or a median, count the share of users who reach value in the first half of the trial: a few stuck accounts can't distort it, and it tells you whether the trial leaves enough time.

What can skew time to value

Time to value only counts users who activate. If slow users give up before getting there, time to value gets shorter while fewer people reach value. Read it next to the activation rate.

Time to value also moves when the audience changes. A campaign that brings a new kind of user can lengthen it on a product that works as before. Compare users from the same acquisition sources: if those also take longer, something in the product got harder.

How to shorten time to value

When many users reach value in the second half of the trial, look at the path to the activation step before the trial length. A setup that takes several sessions, a connection that needs someone else's access, or an activation event placed too deep in the product pushes value late, however long the trial is.

Point new users to one first result rather than a tour of every feature: the one action that shows what the product does, such as a first report or a first message sent. Anything that can wait until after it, like inviting colleagues or setting preferences, should.

Then remove what makes users wait. Sample data or a template lets them see a result before the real connection is in place. When a step depends on someone else, offer help right where users get stuck: a prompt that appears when someone stays idle on the connection page or leaves it, with a way to invite a colleague who has the access, open the setup guide, or contact the team. The same offer works in a usage-based email, sent when someone started the setup but didn't finish it.

What's a good time to value?

There's no benchmark worth quoting, since it depends on the product and on the activation event chosen. A product set up in minutes and one that needs a data connection can't be compared. Aim for most users to get there in the first half of the trial, and follow your own trend, cohort after cohort.

Should the trial last as long as time to value?

It should leave room for it, and for the evaluation that comes after. If most users need eight days to activate, a seven-day trial ends too soon. Making value come sooner usually helps more than adding days, though the trial length also depends on what similar products offer.
