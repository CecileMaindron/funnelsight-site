Moving upmarket: why the enterprise pipeline falls short

Two quarters after moving upmarket, Contractly's pipeline fell short, and the room blamed marketing. The biggest leak was further down.

Contractly is a fictional company, and the figures are illustrative, not measured results.

  • 2×demo requests from target accounts, the following quarter
  • 30% → 75%of target accounts in trial contacted by sales within two weeks
  • 2.8× → 3.6×pipeline coverage, against a 4× target
Company
Contractly, a contract management tool that grew by selling self-serve to small businesses
Asked by
The revenue team, after the quarterly pipeline review
Data used
Last quarter's ad clicks, trials, demo requests and first sales contacts from large companies
New buyers
Legal, procurement and finance teams at large companies

Two quarters after Contractly started selling to large companies, pipeline coverage for the next two quarters stood at 2.8 times the target. The team needs 4, because roughly one opportunity in four closes. The room blamed marketing, and three fixes came up: doubling the paid budget, rewriting the homepage for enterprise buyers, or lowering the MQL bar to give sales more leads.

Before picking one, the team filtered its funnel in Funnelsight on the target accounts, from the campaign to the first sales contact, and asked one question per stage. Each answer was compared with what this new audience should look like, not with the small businesses the team knew.

The new audience, stage by stage

The ads asked buyers for something they don't do. At a small business, the person who finds Contractly is often the one who buys it, and a free trial is the natural next step. At a large company, the buyer is a head of legal or procurement, a role many small businesses don't even have, and they won't set up a tool to judge it. The campaigns reached them and asked them to start a trial. They clicked, and left. Doubling the budget would have bought more of the same clicks.

Lower activity was expected. At a large company, the people who try the product are contract managers and in-house lawyers, often several from the same team. Each of them does part of the work that one person does alone at a small business, so each looks less active. Lowering the MQL bar to small-business levels would have sent sales a queue of what looked like small trials.

The biggest leak was in the handoff. Most of these practitioners didn't ask for a demo. They started a trial, sometimes three or four colleagues from the same company. Read person by person, each looked like a small trial, and sales never saw them. Grouped by account, 35 target companies had three or more people trying the product.

What the team decided

  • OfferAds for buyers

    LinkedIn ads to legal and procurement heads now offer a benchmark of contract turnaround times, with links to sales and to a trial their team can run.

  • RouteTrials, by account

    When three people from a target account start a trial, the account goes to an enterprise rep, with what they've tried, in the CRM.

  • KeepThe trial

    Still the main call to action on the site, now with a "Talk to sales" button beside it. Small businesses still come through it.

The budget stayed where it was, and so did most of the website. In this illustrative scenario, pipeline coverage came back to 3.6 times the target the following quarter. Whether those deals close will show in about six months, the length of the sales cycle.

What to copy

  • Before choosing a fix, ask one question per stage, from "are they signing up?" to "do they close?"
  • Compare a new audience with its own expected values, not with the customers you already have.
  • When the buyer isn't the one who tries the product, offer something to read first, with a link they can pass to their team.
  • Group people by company: a large account rarely arrives as one lead.
