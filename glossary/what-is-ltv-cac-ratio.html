What is the LTV:CAC ratio

The LTV:CAC ratio compares what a customer is worth to your business over time, known as lifetime value or LTV, with what it cost to win that customer, known as customer acquisition cost or CAC. A commonly cited benchmark is 3:1: each customer should bring back roughly three times what it took to acquire them. The right target varies with your business model and sales cycle, and the ratio is only as reliable as the numbers that feed it.

The formula

Formula

LTV:CAC = customer lifetime value ÷ customer acquisition cost

Both halves of that fraction are estimates, and each one hides a few decisions. Most of the mistakes people make with this ratio happen before the division, so the next sections take each half in plain language, one at a time.

Customer lifetime value: what a customer is worth

Lifetime value answers one question: over the whole time someone stays a customer, how much money do they leave you with? Three inputs get you there.

The first is what the customer pays, usually measured as average monthly revenue per customer. The second is gross margin, the share of that revenue left after the direct costs of delivering your product, such as hosting, customer support and payment processing fees. If a customer pays $100 a month and $20 of that goes into serving them, your gross margin is 80%. Marketing and sales salaries don't count here, and neither does product development. The third is how long customers stay, which comes from your monthly churn rate.

Formula

Average customer lifespan in months ≈ 1 ÷ monthly churn rate

Customer lifetime value = (average monthly revenue per customer × gross margin) ÷ monthly churn rate

At 2% monthly churn, the average customer stays about 50 months. Gross margin belongs in the calculation because revenue isn't money you keep. Using revenue in its place is the easiest way to flatter this number: with an 80% margin, it overstates LTV by a quarter.

Two cautions apply. The shortcut above assumes churn stays constant, which it rarely does. And a 50-month lifespan is a prediction, not an observation. If your oldest customers joined 14 months ago, you are extrapolating far beyond your data, so many teams cap the lifespan they assume at a few years and revisit it as history builds up. More rigorous versions also discount future revenue, since a dollar paid in three years is worth less than one paid today. Wall Street Prep's guide walks through that approach with a full worked example.

Customer acquisition cost: what a new customer really costs

Formula

CAC = total sales and marketing spend in a period ÷ number of new paying customers in that period

This looks simple, and most teams still undercount the top number. Ad spend is easy to see, while everything else sits in other budgets and in payroll. A complete figure, often called fully loaded CAC, includes ad spend, the salaries and commissions of everyone in sales and marketing, the tools they use, content production, agency fees and events. Chargebee's guide to CAC goes into what a fully loaded version covers.

Common mistake: counting only paid advertising. A team that spends $10,000 on ads and $30,000 on salaries and tools to win 40 customers hasn't paid $250 per customer. It has paid $1,000, and every number built on top of the lower figure, this ratio included, looks four times better than it should.

The bottom of the fraction needs the same care. Count paying customers only. In a product-led model, thousands of free signups can flow in, but they aren't customers yet, and dividing by them makes acquisition look far cheaper than it is. If you run a free plan or a free trial, the cost of serving those free users is part of what it takes to win the paying ones. Many teams include it in CAC, which is a defensible choice as long as you apply it every time.

Timing matters as well. If your sales cycle runs three months, this quarter's new customers mostly came from last quarter's spend. Use the same period on both sides of the fraction, and shift one of them by your typical sales cycle when that cycle is long.

A worked example

Take a small team selling a plan at $100 a month. Gross margin is 80%, so each customer leaves $80 a month after delivery costs. Monthly churn is 2%, which gives an average lifespan of about 50 months.

Formula

LTV = ($100 × 0.80) ÷ 0.02 = $4,000

Last quarter the team spent $30,000 on sales and marketing, fully loaded, and won 25 new paying customers.

Formula

CAC = $30,000 ÷ 25 = $1,200

LTV:CAC = $4,000 ÷ $1,200 ≈ 3.3

These numbers are illustrative, not a benchmark. A ratio of 3.3 clears the usual 3:1 bar, and the section on payback below shows why that isn't the whole story for this team.

How to read the result

A ratio below 1:1 means you spend more winning a customer than that customer will ever leave you after delivery costs. Between 1:1 and 3:1, the business works but growth is expensive relative to what it returns. From 3:1 upward, acquisition is generally considered healthy.

The reason the bar sits at 3 and not at 1.5 is that LTV is built on gross margin, which hasn't yet paid for product development, management, office space or any support cost beyond the direct ones. The surplus above 1:1 is the cushion that covers them.

A very high ratio isn't automatically good news. Above 5:1 can mean you could grow faster by spending more on acquisition. Before drawing that conclusion, check the measurement: a ratio that looks too good is more often a CAC missing its salaries, or an LTV stretched over a lifespan nobody has observed yet.

When the ratio is low, find out which side is responsible before acting. On the LTV side, the levers are lower churn, better pricing, expansion revenue from existing customers and a higher gross margin. On the CAC side, they are better conversion from visit to signup and from signup to paid, more budget on the channels that actually produce customers, and a shorter sales cycle. Churn deserves particular attention because its effect is large: cutting monthly churn from 3% to 2% stretches the average lifespan from about 33 months to 50 and raises LTV by half.

The ratio is a snapshot, not a verdict. Published benchmarks differ by segment and sources don't agree on exact figures, so read them as a range. Your own trend tells you more than a comparison with someone else's number: a ratio that climbs from 2:1 to 3:1 over a year says something specific about your business.

Payback period: how long until a customer pays for themselves

Formula

CAC payback period in months = CAC ÷ (monthly revenue per customer × gross margin)

The ratio tells you what a customer is worth in the end. Payback tells you how long you wait to get your acquisition spend back, which matters because you pay for acquisition today and collect over years. A business can post a solid ratio and still run short of cash if payback is long and growth keeps demanding new spending.

In the example above, $1,200 ÷ $80 gives 15 months. With 2% monthly churn, about one customer in four has left by then. The ratio says this team is healthy over the long run, and payback says it waits more than a year to see the cash. Both statements are true, and they lead to different decisions.

Many teams like to see payback within about 12 months. The acceptable window stretches with the business model: a self-serve product with monthly plans usually recovers its costs faster than an enterprise deal with a long sales cycle. Customers who pay annually up front pull cash forward, which is one reason some teams offer a discount on annual plans.

Getting started without a full LTV model

You don't need the full model on day one. Four numbers are enough for a first pass: total sales and marketing spend for a recent period, the number of new paying customers in that period, average monthly revenue per customer, and a rough gross margin. From them you get CAC, payback, and a conservative LTV: the gross profit one customer produces in their first 12 months, with no prediction involved. If that figure already exceeds CAC, you recover your acquisition cost within about a year. Add churn and a proper lifespan once you have enough history to trust them.

If you have never calculated gross margin, you're in good company. It lives with finance, and marketing teams rarely see it. Ask whoever produces your profit and loss statement for a figure, or estimate hosting, support and payment fees per customer yourself. A rough margin, labeled as rough, is far better than using revenue.

Why it matters for PLG and hybrid motions

In a self-serve or hybrid PLG motion, CAC is easy to undercount when free trial and marketing costs aren't fully attributed, and LTV is easy to overstate when expansion revenue isn't tracked separately from new business. Expansion works in your favor here: customers who add seats or move to a higher plan raise LTV without any extra acquisition cost. Follow it on its own line with net revenue retention, so a few large upgrades don't hide weak retention among the other accounts.

Teams running a self-serve motion alongside a sales-assisted one should calculate the ratio per motion, not blended, since the two usually have very different acquisition costs and retention profiles.

What is a good LTV:CAC ratio?

3:1 is a commonly cited benchmark, meaning a customer is worth about three times what it cost to acquire them. The right target depends on your business model, sales cycle, and margin structure, so treat it as a starting reference, not a fixed rule.

Can the LTV:CAC ratio be too high?

Yes. A very high ratio can mean you're underspending on acquisition and leaving growth on the table. It can also mean the calculation is off, for example a CAC that leaves out salaries. Review it alongside your growth targets and check the inputs before celebrating.

Should I use revenue or gross margin to calculate LTV?

Gross margin. Revenue includes money you spend delivering the product, so using it overstates what a customer is worth. If you don't know your margin yet, use a rough estimate and label it as one.

What is the difference between LTV:CAC and CAC payback?

The ratio measures how much a customer is worth compared with what they cost to acquire, over their whole lifetime. Payback measures how many months it takes to earn that acquisition cost back. A business can have a healthy ratio and a slow payback, which is why teams track both.

Do I need a full data pipeline to calculate this?

No. You can start with a spreadsheet and four numbers: sales and marketing spend, new paying customers, average monthly revenue per customer, and a rough gross margin. Refine it once churn data is in better shape.
