What is the LTV:CAC ratio?

Updated November 23, 2026

The LTV:CAC ratio compares what a customer brings in over the time they stay, their lifetime value (LTV), with what it cost to win them, the customer acquisition cost (CAC). A ratio of 3:1 means a customer brings in three times what they cost. It answers a practical question: how much can you spend to win a customer, on a given channel or in a given segment?

How to calculate the LTV:CAC ratio

Formulas

LTV:CAC ratio = lifetime value ÷ customer acquisition cost

Customer lifetime value (CLV or LTV) = monthly revenue per customer × gross margin × months a customer stays

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) = sales and marketing spend ÷ new paying customers, over the same period

Two of these inputs usually come from finance. Gross margin is what's left of a customer's payment once the cost of serving them is paid, such as hosting, support and payment fees. The full CAC includes salaries, tools and events, not only ads. If finance can't share them, use rough estimates and say so.

Say a new customer costs $3,000 to win. They pay $250 a month, and $200 of that is left once the cost of serving them is paid. Each month, 2% of customers leave. The usual shortcut divides the monthly margin by the share of customers who leave each month: $200 ÷ 2% = $10,000, a ratio of 3.3:1. That counts every month a customer might ever stay. Count only the months you can observe, and the answer changes.

What is a good LTV:CAC ratio?

A ratio of 3:1 or higher is generally considered good, as long as you know how many months the LTV counts.

LTV:CAC ratio ranges
RatioWhat it usually means
Below 1:1A customer costs more to win than they will ever leave you.
1:1 to 3:1Acquisition pays for itself, with little left for the rest of the business.
3:1 to 5:1Generally considered healthy.
Above 5:1Room to spend more, or an LTV that counts years nobody has seen yet.

The bar sits at 3 rather than 1 because the margin also has to pay for building the product, running the company and everything else that isn't winning or serving customers. Read the ranges against the horizon you used. In the example, 1.7:1 over three years says more than 3.3:1 over a lifetime nobody has seen.

Why lifetime value is the hard half

CAC comes from money you've already spent. LTV depends on how long customers will stay, and on a new product or a new segment, nobody knows that yet. What makes it hard also depends on how you sell.

  • Self-serve and small accounts. Customers move up plans at their own pace. Some grow into a contract handled by sales, or into another of your products, and their revenue ends up counted somewhere else.
  • Contracts with an account team. Revenue changes mostly at renewal, and you only learn the renewal rate as contracts come up for renewal.

Start with payback, the months of margin it takes to earn back what a customer cost. It needs no guess about how long customers stay. Then use a capped LTV, two or three years of margin based on what customers actually paid, and extend the horizon as history builds up. A cautious LTV costs you a little growth. An optimistic one costs you the budget spent on customers who never pay it back.

The capped LTV is also the most a customer can cost before you lose money over that period. In the example, CAC could rise from $3,000 to $5,170 over three years. Costs tend to rise as you spend more on a channel, because the extra budget reaches people further from your target, so the room left matters more than the ratio itself.

Where LTV:CAC goes wrong

CAC and LTV don't count the same costs. Ad spend alone on one side, every month of margin on the other, and the ratio looks better than it is. Say which costs CAC includes, and keep the same ones from one period to the next.

One ratio covers every channel. Each channel has its own CAC, and its customers stay and grow differently. Net revenue retention by source shows how wide that gap can be. A single ratio averages a channel you should grow with one you should cut.

Blended CAC vs paid CAC, and the split B2B needs

Blended CAC divides all acquisition spend by all new customers, whatever brought them in. Paid CAC divides paid media spend by the customers that paid channels brought. The gap between the two shows how much organic search, referrals and word of mouth hold the average down.

In B2B, both are usually calculated on program spend: ads, events, content, tools. Sales salaries and commission plans vary by rep and by deal, so they're rarely in the number. Customers signed by sales cost more than any of these figures shows.

In a hybrid model, the split that matters is between self-serve and sales-assisted customers. When the self-serve share grows, blended CAC falls, even though no customer got cheaper to acquire.

To calculate CAC by sales motion, decide which spend belongs to each motion. Split what serves both, such as trial campaigns that bring self-serve customers and leads for sales, by a rule you keep from one quarter to the next.

Keep in mind that salaries aren't included, and they make a big difference between self-serve and sales-assisted customers. So does LTV, which tends to be higher for sales-assisted customers.

LTV vs CAC: what's the difference?

LTV is what a customer leaves you over the time they stay, after the direct cost of serving them. CAC is what it cost to win them. Compared as a ratio, they show how much room you have to spend. While LTV is still uncertain, compare CAC with payback instead, the months of margin it takes to earn it back.

Is CAC the same as cost per acquisition (CPA)?

Not quite. CPA usually measures what one conversion from a campaign cost, such as a lead, a sign-up or a demo request. CAC measures what it cost to win a paying customer. A campaign can show a low CPA and still bring customers with a high CAC, when few of its leads end up buying.
