What is the LTV:CAC ratio, and how much can you spend to acquire a new customer?

The LTV:CAC ratio compares what a customer is worth over the time they stay, their lifetime value (LTV), with what it cost to win them, the customer acquisition cost (CAC). A ratio of 3:1 is the figure most often cited. In practice, the ratio answers a simple question: how much can you spend to acquire a new customer, on this channel or in this segment? Most teams have a CAC figure, at least for ads. LTV is the hard half.

Formula

LTV:CAC = lifetime value ÷ customer acquisition cost

LTV = monthly revenue per customer × gross margin × months a customer stays

CAC = sales and marketing spend ÷ new paying customers, over the same period

Two of these inputs are often out of a marketer's reach. Gross margin is what's left of a customer's payment once the direct costs of serving them are paid: hosting, support, payment fees. Finance holds that figure. The full CAC includes salaries, tools and events, not only ads, and salaries are often confidential. Ask finance for both, or use rough estimates and label them as such: the reasoning below still works.

Why lifetime value is the hard half

CAC comes from spend you've already made. LTV depends on years you haven't seen yet, and on a new product or a new segment, nobody knows yet how long customers stay. Beyond that, what makes it hard depends on how you sell.

  • Self-serve and small accounts. Customers start on a small plan and move up at their own pace, faster when someone works the account and slower when nobody does. There's no typical curve to project. Some grow into a contract handled by sales, or into another of your products, and their revenue then shows up somewhere else.
  • Contracts with an account team. Revenue moves mostly at renewal, when the account owner sells more usage or a higher tier. Month to month it's more predictable, and the uncertainty sits in the renewal rate, which you only learn as contracts come up for renewal.

One example, three answers

Say a new customer costs $3,000 to win. They pay $250 a month, of which $200 is gross margin. Each month, 2% of customers leave. The usual formula divides the monthly margin by the share of customers who leave each month: $200 ÷ 2% = $10,000, a ratio of 3.3, comfortably above 3:1. That figure counts every month a customer might ever stay. Stop the count at a horizon you can actually observe, and the answer changes.

None of the three figures is wrong. They answer different questions, and the ratio that reaches 3:1 rests on the part you can't check yet.

A lifetime value you can use without a full model

Start with payback: how many months of margin it takes to earn back what a customer cost to win. Here, $3,000 ÷ $200 = 15 months. No guess about how long customers stay is needed. With 2% leaving each month, about three customers in four are still there after 15 months.

Then calculate a capped LTV: the margin customers produce over two or three years, nothing beyond. Base it on what customers actually paid, not on the upgrades you expect. For contracts, use the renewal rates you've already seen. And decide once whether a customer who moves to another plan or product still counts, then stick to it.

As history builds up, extend the horizon. A capped LTV that turns out too cautious costs you a little growth. An optimistic one costs you the budget spent on customers who never pay it back.

How much more can you spend to acquire a customer?

This is the question the ratio is for. With the 36-month LTV of $5,170, a customer can cost up to $5,170 before you lose money over three years. Today's CAC is $3,000, so it can rise by about 70% before that line.

Acquisition costs tend to rise as you spend more on a channel: the extra budget reaches people a little further from your target, at a higher price. So the room left before that line matters more than the ratio itself. A channel with a modest ratio and a stable cost can be a safer place to add budget than one with a high ratio on a lifetime nobody has observed.

Read it by channel, and separate self-serve from sales-led

CAC differs from one channel to the next, and so does how long customers stay and grow: net revenue retention by source shows how wide that gap can be. A blended ratio averages a channel you should grow with one you should cut. Calculate self-serve and sales-led customers separately as well, since both their acquisition costs and their lifetimes differ.

LTV:CAC ratio ranges
RatioWhat it usually means
Below 1:1A customer costs more to win than they will ever leave you.
1:1 to 3:1Acquisition pays for itself, with little left for the rest of the business.
3:1 to 5:1Generally considered healthy.
Above 5:1Room to spend more, or an LTV stretched over years nobody has seen. Check which.

The bar sits at 3 rather than 1 because gross margin still has to pay for product development, management and everything else that isn't acquisition or the direct cost of serving customers. Read the ranges against the horizon you used: 1.7:1 over 36 months says more than 3.3:1 over a lifetime you can't observe.
