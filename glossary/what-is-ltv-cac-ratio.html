What is the LTV:CAC ratio?

Updated November 14, 2026

The LTV:CAC ratio compares what a customer brings in over the time they stay, their lifetime value (LTV), with what it cost to win them, the customer acquisition cost (CAC). A ratio of 3:1 means a customer brings in three times what they cost. It answers a practical question: how much can you spend to win a customer, on a given channel or in a given segment?

Formula

LTV:CAC = lifetime value ÷ customer acquisition cost

LTV = monthly revenue per customer × gross margin × months a customer stays

CAC = sales and marketing spend ÷ new paying customers, over the same period

Two of these inputs usually come from finance. Gross margin is what's left of a customer's payment once the cost of serving them is paid, such as hosting, support and payment fees. The full CAC includes salaries, tools and events, not only ads. If finance can't share them, use rough estimates and say so. The reasoning below still works.

Why lifetime value is the hard half

CAC comes from money you've already spent. LTV depends on how long customers will stay, and on a new product or a new segment, nobody knows that yet. What makes it hard also depends on how you sell.

  • Self-serve and small accounts. Customers move up plans at their own pace. Some grow into a contract handled by sales, or into another of your products, and their revenue ends up counted somewhere else.
  • Contracts with an account team. Revenue changes mostly at renewal, and you only learn the renewal rate as contracts come up for renewal.

One example, three answers

Say a new customer costs $3,000 to win. They pay $250 a month, and $200 of that is left once the cost of serving them is paid. Each month, 2% of customers leave. The usual shortcut divides the monthly margin by the share of customers who leave each month, so $200 ÷ 2% = $10,000, a ratio of 3.3. That counts every month a customer might ever stay. Count only the months you can actually observe, and the answer changes.

None of the three figures is wrong. They answer different questions, and the ratio that reaches 3:1 rests on the part you can't check yet.

What is a good LTV:CAC ratio?

LTV:CAC ratio ranges
RatioWhat it usually means
Below 1:1A customer costs more to win than they will ever leave you.
1:1 to 3:1Acquisition pays for itself, with little left for the rest of the business.
3:1 to 5:1Generally considered healthy.
Above 5:1Room to spend more, or an LTV that counts years nobody has seen yet.

The bar sits at 3 rather than 1 because the margin also has to pay for building the product, running the company and everything else that isn't winning or serving customers. Read the ranges against the horizon you used. In the example, 1.7:1 over three years says more than 3.3:1 over a lifetime nobody has seen.

How to calculate LTV without years of history

Start with payback, the number of months of margin it takes to earn back what a customer cost. Here, $3,000 ÷ $200 = 15 months. It needs no guess about how long customers stay. With 2% leaving each month, about three customers in four are still there after 15 months.

Then calculate a capped LTV, the margin customers bring over two or three years and nothing beyond. Base it on what customers actually paid, not on the upgrades you expect. For contracts, use the renewal rates you've already seen.

As history builds up, extend the horizon. A cautious LTV costs you a little growth. An optimistic one costs you the budget spent on customers who never pay it back.

How much more can you spend to acquire a customer?

This is the question the ratio is for. With the three-year LTV of $5,170, a customer can cost up to $5,170 before you lose money over three years. Today's CAC is $3,000, so it can rise by about 70% before that line.

Costs tend to rise as you spend more on a channel, because the extra budget reaches people further from your target. So the room left matters more than the ratio itself. A channel with a modest ratio and a stable cost can be a safer place for more budget than one with a high ratio on a lifetime nobody has observed.

Where LTV:CAC goes wrong

CAC and LTV don't count the same costs. Ad spend alone on one side, every month of margin on the other, and the ratio looks better than it is. Compare LTV with the full CAC, and keep the ad-only figure for comparing campaigns.

One ratio covers every channel. Each channel has its own CAC, and its customers stay and grow differently. Net revenue retention by source shows how wide that gap can be. A blended ratio averages a channel you should grow with one you should cut. Self-serve and sales-led customers need separate ratios too.

What's the difference between LTV and CAC?

LTV is what a customer leaves you over the time they stay, after the direct cost of serving them. CAC is what it cost to win them. Compared as a ratio, they show how much room you have to spend. While LTV is still uncertain, compare CAC with payback instead, the months of margin it takes to earn it back.

How do you calculate customer acquisition cost (CAC)?

Divide sales and marketing spend by the number of new paying customers over the same period. Decide first what goes in. Ad spend alone gives a paid CAC, useful to compare campaigns. Adding salaries, tools and events gives the full CAC, the one to compare with LTV.
