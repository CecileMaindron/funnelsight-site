How to calculate net revenue retention (NRR)

Total ARR can keep rising while the customer base underneath it erodes. New customers, a price increase or a favorable exchange rate all lift the total. Net revenue retention (NRR) isolates the customers you already had: how much of their recurring revenue they still bring in a year later, upgrades and downgrades included, with new customers left out. Some companies call it net dollar retention (NDR): the calculation is the same.

Formula

NRR (%) = (starting revenue + expansion − contraction − churned revenue) ÷ starting revenue × 100

Above 100%, the customers you already have grow your revenue on their own. Below 100%, new customers first have to make up for what the existing base lost before the total grows at all. Healthy growth needs all three: revenue from new customers, expansion, and customers who stay.

NRR and GRR: the same customers, without the expansion

Gross revenue retention (GRR), also called gross dollar retention, takes the same customers over the same period and leaves expansion out. It can't go above 100%.

Formula

GRR (%) = (starting revenue − contraction − churned revenue) ÷ starting revenue × 100

In the example, GRR = ($10.0M − $0.4M − $0.9M) ÷ $10.0M = 87%. The 16 points between 87% and 103% are expansion. Read the two together. If NRR holds while GRR falls, growth from some customers is covering rising losses from others, and the total won't show it until the expansion slows down.

What goes in each line

  • Expansion: more seats, a higher plan, add-ons. A price increase counts too, but note it separately. It lifts NRR the year it applies, then the effect stops: an NRR of 108% with 6 points from a price increase doesn't say the same thing as 108% from customers adding users. The same goes for a first-year discount that shrinks at renewal: revenue goes up without the customer using more. Watch GRR at that renewal, when customers decide whether the product is worth its full price.
  • Contraction: fewer seats or a lower plan, on an account that stays.
  • Churned revenue: accounts that cancel, counted when the revenue stops, not when the customer gives notice.
  • Left out: one-time fees such as setup or services, customers signed during the period, and currency effects. Calculate at constant exchange rates, or a falling currency makes customers who changed nothing look like contraction.

If monthly and annual contracts are both in the mix, bring them to the same period first: monthly revenue times twelve, next to annual contract values.

Read it by segment, then by source

A single NRR mixes customers who grow with customers who leave. A few large accounts expanding can cover a long tail of small ones leaving: the number looks fine while the base underneath it doesn't. Split it first by segment, such as self-serve and sales-led or contract size, and look at the largest accounts one by one, down to how many of their users are still active (see user-level vs account-level analytics).

Then split it by the source that brought the customers. That's where NRR concerns marketing.

A channel's value doesn't stop at the first year of revenue. Two channels can pay back their cost in the same number of months and be worth very different amounts two years later. Before moving budget between channels, read their NRR next to their payback by source. Customers who leave in their first months weigh on the number too: how to calculate churn rate covers that part.

What is a good NRR?

It depends mostly on contract size, because larger customers have more room to add seats and teams. SaaS Capital's 2025 retention benchmarks for private B2B SaaS companies put median NRR at 102% for contracts of $25k to $50k a year, with the bottom quarter at 97% and the top quarter at 111%. Compare yourself with companies selling at a similar contract size, and above all with your own trend over the last few years.

Should NRR be calculated monthly or annually?

Usually annually, on the customers active at the start of a twelve-month period. A monthly NRR is possible, but small monthly movements add up: a monthly NRR of 99% gives about 89% over a year. Annual contracts also renew on fixed dates, which makes monthly figures jump around.

What is net revenue churn?

Net revenue churn is the mirror of NRR: (churned revenue + contraction − expansion) ÷ starting revenue, or 100% minus NRR. When expansion outweighs the losses, it turns negative, which is why an NRR above 100% is also called negative churn. In the example on this page, net revenue churn is −3%.

What's the difference between MRR and ARR?

MRR is the recurring revenue you bill in a month, and ARR the same revenue over a year. For subscriptions, ARR = MRR × 12. Count only recurring revenue: setup fees and services stay out. Self-serve businesses, where most customers pay a monthly subscription, usually track MRR. Sales-led businesses with annual subscriptions usually report ARR. If you have both, convert one into the other before comparing them.
