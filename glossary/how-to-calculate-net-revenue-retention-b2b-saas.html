How to calculate net revenue retention (NRR)

Updated November 16, 2026

Net revenue retention (NRR) is the share of recurring revenue you keep from the customers you already had a year earlier, counting their upgrades and downgrades and leaving new customers out. It's also called net dollar retention (NDR) or net retention rate, and the calculation is the same.

It answers a question total revenue can hide. Annual recurring revenue (ARR, the subscription revenue over a year) can keep rising while the existing customer base shrinks, as long as new customers or a price increase make up for it.

NRR formula

NRR (%) = (starting revenue + expansion − contraction − churned revenue) ÷ starting revenue × 100

Above 100%, the customers you already have grow your revenue on their own. Below 100%, new customers first have to make up for what the existing base lost before the total grows at all.

NRR vs GRR: same customers, without expansion

Gross revenue retention (GRR), also called gross dollar retention, takes the same customers and leaves expansion out. It shows what you keep before any upgrade, so it can't go above 100%.

GRR formula

GRR (%) = (starting revenue − contraction − churned revenue) ÷ starting revenue × 100

In the example, GRR = ($10.0M − $0.4M − $0.9M) ÷ $10.0M = 87%. The 16 points between 87% and 103% come from upgrades. Read the two together. An NRR that holds while GRR falls means upgrades are covering more and more customers who leave.

What is a good NRR?

It depends mostly on contract size, because larger customers have more room to add seats and teams. In SaaS Capital's 2025 benchmarks for private B2B SaaS companies selling contracts of $25k to $50k a year, half are above 102%, and most sit between 97% and 111%. Compare yourself with companies selling at a similar contract size, and above all with your own trend.

What goes in each line

  • Expansion. More seats, a higher plan, add-ons, and price increases.
  • Contraction. Fewer seats or a lower plan, from a customer who stays.
  • Churned revenue. Customers who cancel, counted when their revenue stops, not when they give notice.
  • Left out. One-time fees such as setup or services, and customers signed during the year.

Where NRR goes wrong

A price increase passes for customers growing. It counts as expansion, but it lifts NRR only the year it applies. An NRR of 108% with 6 points from a price increase doesn't mean what 108% from customers adding users means. When you read an NRR, ask how much of the expansion came from prices.

New customers slip into the calculation. The list of customers is fixed on the first day of the period. A customer signed in March who upgrades in June shouldn't count, or the figure looks better than it is.

Read NRR by segment, then by source

A single NRR mixes customers who grow with customers who leave. A few large accounts expanding can cover a long tail of small ones leaving, and the number looks fine while the base underneath it doesn't. Split it first by segment, such as self-serve and sales-led or contract size, and check the largest accounts one by one (see user-level vs account-level analytics).

Then split it by the source that brought the customers. That's where NRR concerns marketing.

A channel's value doesn't stop at the first year of revenue. Two channels can pay back their cost in the same number of months and be worth very different amounts two years later. Before moving budget between channels, read their NRR next to their payback by source.

Should NRR be calculated monthly or annually?

Usually annually, on the customers active at the start of a twelve-month period. A monthly NRR is possible, but small monthly movements add up: a monthly NRR of 99% gives about 89% over a year. Annual contracts also renew on fixed dates, which makes monthly figures jump around.

What is net revenue churn, and how do you calculate it?

Net revenue churn is the mirror of NRR. It measures what the customers you already had took away, net of their upgrades.

Net revenue churn formula

Net revenue churn (%) = (churned revenue + contraction − expansion) ÷ starting revenue × 100

It equals 100% minus NRR. When upgrades outweigh the losses, it turns negative, which is why an NRR above 100% is also called negative churn. In the example on this page, net revenue churn is −3%.

What's the difference between MRR and ARR?

MRR is the recurring revenue you bill in a month, and ARR the same revenue over a year. For subscriptions, ARR = MRR × 12. Self-serve businesses, where most customers pay monthly, usually track MRR. Sales-led businesses with annual contracts usually report ARR.
