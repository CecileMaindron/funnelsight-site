What is a product qualified lead (PQL)?

Updated November 25, 2026

In sales and marketing, PQL means product qualified lead. It's a person or a team whose use of your product shows they're ready for a sales conversation, most often during a free trial or on a free plan. An MQL is qualified by how someone engaged with your marketing. A PQL is qualified by what they did in the product.

How to measure PQLs

Three numbers tell you whether PQLs work. The PQL rate shows how many trials become one, and the PQL-to-customer rate how many of those buy. Time to purchase shows whether they also sign sooner. Compare the last two with the trials that never became PQLs.

Formula

PQL rate (%) = (new trials that became PQLs ÷ new trials) × 100

PQL-to-customer rate (%) = (PQLs who became paying customers ÷ PQLs) × 100

Time to purchase = days from trial start to payment, PQLs compared with other trials

Count by cohort rather than by calendar month. Take the trials that started in a given week, and count how many reached the signal within the trial. Otherwise someone who starts on the 30th and qualifies on the 5th lands in the wrong month, and the rate swings for no real reason.

Why add PQLs if every trial can already be an MQL?

A trial sign-up shows that someone wants to try the product, but nothing yet about what they'll do with it. A few days later, some are already using it for real work, some are stuck on setup, and some haven't logged in since. Handled as one list of MQLs, they all get the same follow-up at the same pace, and the rep calls each of them knowing just as little.

The PQL is lead scoring based on product use. Where an MQL score looks at who the person is and which emails they opened, the PQL looks at what they did in the product. Sales calls those people first, while they're active, and the other trials still get the usual follow-up. They've already seen the product work for them, so the conversation can start further along.

How to define a PQL from what paying customers did in their trial

Start from the outcome and work backward. Take the trials that turned into paying customers over the last few months, and the trials from the same months that didn't. Look at what each group did during the trial. You're looking for an action that shows up often among those who paid and rarely among the others. Unlike activation, which every new user should reach, a PQL signal is one that only part of the trials reach, mostly those who go on to buy.

The trial sets the limit. In 14 days, many trial users only come back on a handful of days, so the signal has to be reachable in that window. A threshold that takes a month of use never fires, and good leads buy or leave before sales hears about them. On a free plan there's no deadline, and the question moves to timing: the moment someone reaches a limit of the free plan is often the moment to talk.

You rarely find the right signal on the first attempt. Five are commonly used:

  • Repeated use. Coming back on several days. Easy to measure, but someone can return often without touching what matters.
  • Using the core feature. Repeating the action behind your activation event, past a threshold. Slack's 2,000-message mark is the best-known threshold of this kind: almost all the teams that passed it kept using the product.
  • Reaching a limit. Hitting a cap on seats, projects or usage. It puts a decision in front of the user, if your plan has limits.
  • Bringing others in. Inviting colleagues, or several people active on the same trial. A strong sign when buying is a group decision.
  • Looking at plans in the product. Opening the plans or billing page inside the product. It's close to a decision, so it often comes late. The pricing page on your website counts for less, since many trial users pass through it on their way to sign up.

The stricter the signal, the shorter and later the list. If sales is flooded with leads who go nowhere, tighten it. If the list is almost empty, loosen it. Within a trial, a burst of key actions over two or three days is usually a better moment to call than the total since the trial began.

Whatever the signal, check the account first. A business email, a company in your target and real data connected instead of a sample file tell you the trial is a real evaluation. The job title weighs less than in an MQL score, because the person testing is often not the one who buys. A developer or an analyst asked to evaluate the tool is a good sign for the account, even if they never sign the contract. A student on a personal email, alone, with sample data, is curiosity, however active. Put together, the signal and the size of the account sort a week of trials into four groups.

How sales should follow up on a PQL

A PQL is as perishable as a demo request. What flagged it happened in the last day or two and is still on the user's mind, so it belongs in the same queue as the hottest leads, not in a weekly list.

The conversation is different, though. A PQL didn't ask to talk to anyone. The user is in the middle of trying the product and is more open to help with it than to a pitch. The rep opens with what the team did and something useful for their use case, which means knowing the product well enough to talk about it, or bringing someone who does. When the active user isn't the buyer, the call is also a way to find who decides. Questions about who else is involved and what a rollout would take come once the help has landed.

Sales will doubt the first lists, and fairly: a long list without context looks like any other lead. Start with fewer PQLs, from accounts large enough for a call to matter. Then check that the call helps: for a few weeks, follow up half of comparable PQLs and leave the other half alone, and compare conversion, plan and time to purchase.

The follow-up doesn't end at the first call either. New colleagues invited, or a return to the plans page while the deal is open, tells the rep something the CRM doesn't show yet.

Common PQL mistakes

The bar sits in the wrong place. A rising PQL rate with a falling PQL-to-customer rate often means the bar is too low, and sales soon stops trusting the list. A very high conversion on a tiny list can mean the bar only catches people who were going to buy anyway.

A large account looks hotter than it is. Many people testing in the first week make a large company look ready to buy. Its signature still goes through procurement and a security review.

The definition changes without a date. Review it every quarter, and date each change, so a jump in the rate isn't mistaken for a change in your trials.

PQL vs MQL: what's the difference?

An MQL is qualified by who someone is and how they engaged with your marketing, such as downloading a guide or requesting a demo. A PQL is qualified by what they did in the product. The same person can be both. Count them once, and keep both origins on the record, so marketing and sales report the same total.

PQL vs activated user: what's the difference?

Every new user should reach activation, the first time the product does its job for them. Only part of them become PQLs. A PQL adds what activation doesn't show, an account in your target and signs of buying intent, such as reaching a limit or inviting colleagues.

PQL vs SQL: what's the difference?

A PQL is qualified by what someone did in the product, before anyone has spoken to them. An SQL has been qualified through a conversation, by a BDR or a sales rep depending on how your team is organized. A PQL can become an SQL once that conversation confirms there's a real opportunity. Stage names vary between companies, so follow the ones your CRM already uses.

Do you need a product analytics tool for PQL scoring?

Not to start. Product events usually already sit in your application database or your data warehouse, and a weekly export compared with the deals in your CRM is enough to test a definition. Once it holds, the PQL becomes a field in the CRM, often updated by the same sync as your MQL scores. Combine several signals into a score only when one already leads to real conversations.

Do you always need PQLs in a product-led model?

No. PQLs help sales decide which trials to call first. Some models don't need that choice. When the product is easy to adopt alone and the price too low for a sales call to pay off, trials convert on their own, and activation and trial-to-paid are enough to follow. When trial volume is low enough for sales to follow up on every trial, there is no order to set. Product use then helps sales prepare each call rather than decide which ones to make.
