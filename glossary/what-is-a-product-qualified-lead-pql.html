What is a product qualified lead (PQL), and how to put it to work

A product qualified lead (PQL) is a person or a team whose use of your product, usually during a free trial, shows they're worth a sales conversation now. It adds a second way to qualify a lead next to the MQL: what someone did in the product, instead of what they read or downloaded. Defining one takes a sentence. Getting sales to act on it takes more work.

Define the signal from what paying customers did in their trial

Start from the outcome and work backward. Take the trials that turned into paying customers over the last few months, and the trials from the same months that didn't. Look at what each group did during the trial. You're looking for an action that shows up often among those who paid and rarely among the others. It follows the same logic as defining activation, one step closer to the purchase.

The trial sets the limit. In 14 days, most people use the product on a handful of days, so the signal has to be reachable in that window. A threshold that takes a month of use never fires, and good leads buy or leave before sales hears about them. On a free plan there's no deadline, and the question moves to timing: the moment someone reaches a limit of the free plan is often the moment to talk.

You rarely find the right signal on the first attempt. Five are commonly used, from the broadest to the most telling:

  1. Repeated use

    Coming back on several days. Easy to measure, but someone can return often without touching what matters.

  2. Using the core feature

    Completing the action your paying customers repeat, usually your activation event.

  3. Reaching a limit

    Hitting a cap on seats, projects or usage. It puts a decision in front of the user, if your plan has limits.

  4. Bringing others in

    Inviting colleagues, or several people active on the same trial. A strong sign when buying is a group decision.

  5. Looking at plans and pricing

    Visiting the pricing or billing pages. The closest signal to a decision, and for that reason it often comes late.

The higher the signal, the shorter and later the list. If sales is flooded with leads who go nowhere, move up a rung. If the list is almost empty, step down.

Whatever the signal, check the account first. A business email, a company in your target and real data connected instead of a sample file tell you the trial is a real evaluation. The job title weighs less than in an MQL score, because the person testing is often not the one who buys. A developer or an analyst asked to evaluate the tool is a good sign for the account, even if they never sign the contract. A student on a personal email, alone, with sample data, is curiosity, however active.

PQL scoring: do you need a product analytics tool?

Not to start. Product events usually already sit in your application database or your data warehouse. A weekly export, compared with the deals in your CRM, is enough to test a definition for a few weeks before automating anything.

Once the definition holds, the PQL becomes a field on the contact or account record in the CRM. The same sync that already feeds your MQL scores can update it, often through the warehouse, every hour or every day. Real time is rarely needed. A trial lasts 14 days, and a call the next morning is still in time.

Combining several signals into a PQL score is worth it once one signal already leads to real conversations. Give most of the points to behavior. Give extra weight to a burst of key actions over two or three days: that's usually the moment to call, more than the total since the trial began. And watch for one false alarm. A large company with many people testing looks very hot in the first week, but its signature still goes through procurement and a security review.

MQLs and PQLs in the same funnel: aligning marketing goals

The same person can be both: they download a guide, then start a trial. Count them once, and keep both origins visible on the record. Otherwise each team claims the same lead and the totals stop adding up.

Set the goal further down than the lead. Raising the number of MQLs or PQLs is the easy part. The number that means something is the first sales conversation they lead to, or the purchase for customers who buy on their own. It's far enough down the funnel to signal a real opportunity, and early enough to correct course before the pipeline runs dry.

Getting sales to follow up on PQLs

That first conversation depends on sales picking up the PQL. Two objections come up again and again, and both are fair.

The first: "a PQL is just another lead." If the list is long and comes with no context, sales will treat it like a guide download, and they'll be right. Send fewer PQLs, only from accounts in your target, with what the team did in the product: a source connected, three colleagues invited, two visits to the pricing page. The first call then starts from what they did. When the active user isn't the buyer, the PQL is a reason to reach the account and find who decides.

The second: "they'll buy online anyway." Sometimes that's true. Check it before arguing: for a few weeks, send half of comparable PQLs to sales and leave the other half to buy on their own, then compare conversion, plan and time to purchase. If a call changes nothing for small accounts, stop routing them, and keep sales for the accounts where a conversation changes the result. The self-serve customers your sales team can't see covers what that split looks like in the CRM.

Where the PQL goes decides what you watch
Where the PQL goesWhat to watch
To salesThe share of PQLs that get a first conversation within a few days, then the PQL-to-customer rate. A PQL nobody contacted tells you nothing about the definition.
Left to buy on their ownThe share of PQLs that buy by the end of the trial, and how long they take. PQLs that stall at the end of the trial are the ones a short message can still help.

Measure whether it works: conversion and speed

A PQL can pay off in two ways: more trials turn into customers, or they sign sooner. Track both, against the trials that never became PQLs.

Formula

PQL rate (%) = (new trials that became PQLs ÷ new trials) × 100

PQL-to-customer rate (%) = (PQLs who became paying customers ÷ PQLs) × 100

Time to purchase = days from trial start to payment, PQLs compared with other trials

Count by cohort rather than by calendar month. Take the trials that started in a given week, and count how many reached the signal within the trial. Otherwise someone who starts on the 30th and qualifies on the 5th lands in the wrong month, and the rate swings for no real reason.

Read the numbers together. A rising PQL rate with a falling PQL-to-customer rate usually means the bar is too low. A very high PQL-to-customer rate on a tiny list can mean the bar is so high that you only find people who were going to buy anyway. Review the definition every quarter, and date each change, so a jump in the rate isn't mistaken for a change in your trials.

Is a PQL the same as an activated user?

Not always. Activation marks the point where a user first gets the product's core value. A PQL is a narrower signal built on top of it, one that suggests buying intent, such as reaching a limit or inviting colleagues. Many teams define their first PQL as "activated plus one more sign of intent."
