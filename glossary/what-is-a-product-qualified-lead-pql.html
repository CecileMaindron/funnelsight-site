What is a product qualified lead (PQL)

A product qualified lead (PQL) is a user who has taken specific actions inside your product that show they're likely ready to buy or upgrade. Unlike a marketing qualified lead, which is based on form fills or content downloads, a PQL is defined by real usage: hitting a feature limit, inviting teammates, or reaching an activation milestone. It tells your sales team who to talk to, and when.

Where the term comes from and how it's used

PQLs grew out of product-led growth (PLG), where people try the product through a free trial or a free plan before speaking to anyone, and the product does part of the selling. Instead of sales reps guessing who might be interested, in-product behavior does the qualifying. The user has already tried the thing, and hasn't just said they're interested.

What counts as a PQL varies by product. A project management tool might flag an account once three or more people are actively assigning tasks. A data tool might flag one once a user connects a live source instead of importing a sample file. There's no universal threshold: the definition has to match how your product actually delivers value. That is why defining activation clearly usually comes before defining a PQL. A PQL is often built on top of an activation event, not separate from it.

How a PQL differs from an MQL or an SQL

A marketing qualified lead, or MQL, is someone who engaged with your marketing: they downloaded a guide, attended a webinar or visited several pages. A sales qualified lead, or SQL, is someone a salesperson has vetted on fit, budget and timing. Both signal interest, mostly inferred. A PQL signals use, which you can observe.

Use alone isn't enough, though. Behavior tells you when someone is ready, and fit tells you who they are. A student exploring your free plan every day, or a one-person company far outside your target, can look like a perfect PQL on usage alone. A basic fit check, such as company size or role, can sit on top of the behavior.

Find your signal by working backward from paying customers

The most reliable way to choose a PQL signal is to start from the outcome you want and work backward. Take the customers who converted to a paid plan over the last several months, and the free users who signed up in the same months and didn't convert. Look at what each group did in its first days and weeks. You're searching for an action that shows up often among those who paid and rarely among those who didn't. That action is a candidate signal, whatever it happens to be. It follows the same logic as defining activation, applied one step closer to the purchase.

A spreadsheet export from your product data is enough to start, reviewed weekly rather than tracked live. Two cautions apply. With only a handful of paying customers, treat the pattern as a hypothesis and check it again as the numbers grow. And a signal that predicts conversions doesn't prove that pushing users toward it creates more of them. Some people were going to buy anyway.

Common mistake: calling every free signup or trial start a PQL. A signup shows that someone was curious on one day. If the PQL list is as long as the signup list, sales learns to ignore it and the label stops meaning anything.

Climb from simple signals to more telling ones

You don't need the perfect signal on the first attempt. Begin with something simple that you can measure reliably, check it against what really happens to those users, and move toward more telling behavior as you learn. ProductLed's guide to PQLs describes the same idea: a deliberately simple first definition, such as a number of logins, refined by looking at what those users actually come back to do.

Five signals are commonly used, roughly from the broadest to the most revealing:

  1. Repeated use

    Returning several days in a row, or reaching a number of sessions. Easy to measure and a fine first filter, but it says little about value: a user can come back often without ever touching the feature that matters.

  2. Using the core feature

    Completing the action your best customers repeat, which is usually your activation event. This is where the backward search above pays off.

  3. Reaching a limit

    Hitting a cap on the free plan, such as seats, projects or usage. It shows growing reliance and puts a decision in front of the user. It only applies if your free plan has limits.

  4. Bringing others in

    Inviting teammates, or having several people active in the same workspace. A strong sign when buying is a group decision. It doesn't apply if one admin runs the tool for people who never log in.

  5. Looking at plans and pricing

    Visiting the pricing or billing pages, or asking about plans. This is the closest signal to a decision and, for that reason, it can arrive late.

The higher you climb, the more convincing the signal, and the shorter and later the list. Lower rungs hand sales plenty of leads who are mostly not ready. Higher rungs hand them a few leads who are nearly decided, and sales may add little. If your team is overwhelmed by leads who go nowhere, move up a rung. If the list is almost empty, step down. Only once one signal is driving real conversations is it worth combining several into a scoring model, where each behavior earns points toward a threshold.

Who counts as the lead depends on your product. If several people use it, one enthusiastic user rarely decides alone, so it often makes sense to qualify the whole account. If one admin or developer sets it up for people who never log in, look at what that person puts in place, such as a live integration. User-level vs account-level analytics explains the difference.

Measure it with the PQL rate

Once a definition exists, two numbers tell you whether it works. The PQL rate shows how many new signups reach the signal. The PQL-to-customer rate shows how many of those PQLs end up paying.

Formula

PQL rate (%) = (new signups who became PQLs ÷ new signups) × 100

PQL-to-customer rate (%) = (PQLs who became paying customers ÷ PQLs) × 100

Count by cohort rather than by calendar month alone. Take the people who signed up in a given week or month, and count how many reached the signal within a fixed window, say 30 days. Otherwise someone who signs up on the 30th and qualifies on the 5th lands in the wrong period, and the rate swings for no real reason.

Read the two numbers together. A rising PQL rate with a falling PQL-to-customer rate usually means the bar is too low and you are catching curiosity. A very high PQL-to-customer rate on a tiny list can point the other way: the bar is so high that you only find people who were going to buy anyway. There's no universal good number. Figures published online are rarely sourced and depend on pricing, product and market, so your own trend over time is the comparison that matters.

Make it a shared definition

A PQL definition that lives in one team's head rarely survives. Product usually holds the data and knows what each action means. Marketing uses the definition to decide who to reach and what to say. Sales decides which leads deserve a conversation, and is best placed to tell the others when the list is weak. Someone also has to make the usage data available, which often falls to engineering.

Agree on the handoff in advance, in a few written lines. Say who is notified when a PQL appears and how quickly they follow up. Say what context goes with the lead, and what happens when it turns out to be a poor fit. The context matters most. A message that mentions what the user actually did in the product lands very differently from a generic "upgrade now" email.

Give each team one volume number and one quality number, so nobody improves their own metric at another team's expense. Marketing might watch signups and signup-to-PQL rate, and sales the PQLs contacted and the PQL-to-customer rate. Then review the definition on a schedule. Every quarter is a reasonable rhythm, since the product keeps changing underneath it.

Is a PQL the same as an activated user?

Not always. Activation usually marks the point where a user first experiences the product's core value. A PQL is a narrower signal built on top of that, one that specifically suggests buying intent, like hitting a usage limit or adding teammates. Many teams define their first PQL as "activated plus one more sign of intent."

Who owns the PQL definition, marketing or sales?

It works best as a shared definition. Marketing and product usually identify the behavior, and sales confirms it's a meaningful signal worth acting on. If only one side defines it, the other side tends to ignore the resulting lead list.

How is a PQL different from an MQL?

An MQL is a lead who engaged with your marketing, for example by downloading a guide. A PQL is a user who has used your product in a way that suggests they're ready to buy. The first signals interest, the second signals use you can observe.

What is a PQL rate and how do I calculate it?

The PQL rate is the share of new signups who reach your PQL signal. Divide the number of new signups who became PQLs by the number of new signups, then multiply by 100. Counting by signup cohort, rather than by calendar month, avoids distortions when people qualify days after they sign up.

How many signals do I need to define a PQL?

One is enough to start. Pick the behavior that best separates customers who paid from users who didn't, check it against real conversions, and add more signals only once the first one is driving real conversations.
