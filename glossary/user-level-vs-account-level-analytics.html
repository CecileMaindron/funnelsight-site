User-level vs account-level analytics

A sales rep asks for a report by account. What they want to know is whether the company is worth their time, and who to call. The two answers sit at two different levels.

Start from what you want to know

The question decides where a report starts and where it ends. Most commercial questions need both user-level and account-level data, in a different order.

What you want to knowStart fromEnd with
Which companies to work onAccounts: how many people, how active, which stageThe teams involved inside each one
Who to call in an accountThe account's contacts and usersThe one whose role and activity fit the offer
Which campaigns workThe leads each campaign broughtThe opportunities on their accounts
Whether a customer is at riskThe account's usage and renewal dateThe users who stopped being active

When several people from one team read the site, download content or sign up for a trial within a few days, there's often a project or a training behind it. Either way, it's a good moment to reach out, and twenty contacts attached to an account won't tell a rep which one to contact first.

Who the person is changes what the activity means. A university account often brings a stream of student sign-ups that is easy to dismiss as a whole, yet the same email domain can hide someone from the IT department evaluating the product for the campus. The reverse happens too: a CTO who downloads a white paper looks like the obvious contact, but next to an IT manager who has tested the product all week, the CTO is someone to bring in later, and the IT manager is the person to reach now.

The same choice shapes the message

Marketing faces the same choice. Data at the account level lets a campaign speak to the company as a whole: why the project matters to the business, which teams are already involved, what each person weighing the decision needs to hear. That's what account-based marketing is for, and it only pays off when it serves a sales objective.

Data at the person level lets the message follow what someone has actually done: the guide they read, the feature they tried, the page they keep coming back to. A follow-up that picks up from there usually lands better than one written for a job title alone.

Before you trust an account view

People sign up with personal email addresses, subsidiaries use their own domains, and one group can appear as several accounts in the CRM. Each of these splits a company into fragments or leaves people outside it.

The account itself can mislead. When a product is used by one department, the CRM account still covers the whole group, so a few active users can look like a small footprint in a large company, or like broad adoption when they all sit in one team. Read the account together with the teams behind it.

A grouped signal still needs context. A partner may start a trial to test the product for a client, an existing customer may sign up its staff for trials to train them, and a department may not know its company already holds licences. Each looks like new interest, and each calls for a different conversation.

What is account-level reporting?

Account-level reporting groups leads, contacts and product users under the company they belong to, and reports on companies rather than people: how many accounts reached each stage, which ones are active, how many people each one involves. It answers which companies to work on. Who to contact inside them takes the person-level view underneath.

How do you match leads and users to the right account?

Most teams start with the email domain, then fill the gaps: enrichment data for people who sign up with a personal address, the CRM's parent and child accounts for subsidiaries, and a manual review of the leads that remain unmatched. Agree on the rules once, so marketing and sales count the same accounts.
