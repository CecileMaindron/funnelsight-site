What counts as user activation in product-led growth

Activation is the moment a new user experiences the value your product promised, not the moment they create an account. Sign-up and first login are the usual defaults, because they are easy to count. Neither tells you whether the product actually worked for that person.

How to calculate the activation rate

Once the activation event is chosen, the calculation is simple:

Formula

Activation rate (%) = (number of users who completed the activation event ÷ total new sign-ups) × 100

Teams rarely disagree on the formula. They disagree on the event in the numerator.

Why track activation

Before a trial can convert, the user has to use the product. A low activation rate is an early warning, weeks before conversion figures are in. The message may have brought the wrong people, the product may not fit that segment, or onboarding may lose users before their first useful result.

Activation doesn't guarantee a sale. Some customers buy without touching the trial, and some contracts renew with little use. But users who never activate are much less likely to pay or stay.

How to define activation for your product

No event counts as activation across every product. For a project management tool, it might mean inviting a teammate and assigning a task. For an analytics tool, connecting a data source and viewing a first real report. Start from retention, not from your onboarding flow, and work backward:

  1. Start from the users who stayed

    Take the accounts that kept using the product and became customers, not the ones who finished onboarding.

  2. Find the action they share

    Look for the action, or short sequence of actions, that shows up consistently among them in their first days.

  3. Check it against the users who left

    A good activation event is rare among accounts that churned. If everyone does it, it doesn't separate anything.

  4. Prefer a repeated action to a single step

    A completed setup step is rarely enough. The first action that resembles everyday use of the product is usually a better signal, and feature adoption rate shows how to read each feature against revenue, from trial to renewal.

Slack is the best-known example. Its team found that almost all the teams that had exchanged around 2,000 messages kept using the product. A strong activation event is something your best-retained accounts do repeatedly, not something everyone does once during sign-up. Wes Bush's ProductLed Playbook breaks this down further if you want the full framework.

Where activation numbers go wrong

Each team counts its own. Product tracks feature use in the analytics tool, growth builds cohorts from exports, sales watches logins. None of the numbers agree, and the definition, written once in a slide, is never revisited.

Activation becomes the sales signal. Reaching the activation event shows the product worked for someone, not that they're ready to buy. Sending every activated user to sales floods reps with people who are simply using the product. A product qualified lead usually adds signs of buying intent, such as several colleagues invited or repeated visits to the pricing page.

The definition changes without a date. A feature moves, onboarding changes, a new plan adds a different first step, and the event that made sense a year ago no longer fits. Revisit it when the product changes, not every time the rate dips. When it does dip, time to value helps tell a change in who signs up from a product that got harder. Date each change, so the break isn't read as a trend.

Is activation the same as completing onboarding?

No. Onboarding completion measures whether someone finished a set of steps you designed. Activation measures whether they experienced real value. A user can finish onboarding and never activate.

What's the difference between activation and adoption?

Activation happens once, the first time a new user gets real value from the product, often during the trial. Adoption comes after. It describes how widely and how regularly the product, or one of its features, is used over time. The activation rate tells you whether new users reach that first value, and the adoption rate whether they keep coming back to it.

How soon after sign-up should activation happen?

Before the trial ends. Look at when your retained accounts reached the event. If most did it within three days, a user still not there by day seven needs attention, and a trial much shorter than that window cuts off users who would have activated.

Do I need engineering help to track activation?

Not to define the event or follow it through the funnel in Funnelsight, if your product analytics tool already records the action. If it doesn't, recording it is a short, one-time setup, standard for any analytics tool.
