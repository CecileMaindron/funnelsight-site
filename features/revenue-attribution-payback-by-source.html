What each source is worth, and how fast it pays back

The source that brings the most leads is rarely the one that brings the most revenue. Funnelsight shows, for each source, the recurring revenue its customers generate and how many months that revenue takes to cover what the source cost, so budget discussions start from what a channel is worth rather than how loud it is.

How payback is calculated

Formula

Payback (months) = cost of the source over the period ÷ new monthly recurring revenue from the customers it brought

Revenue comes from the deals recorded in your CRM, whether monthly plans, multi-year contracts or renewals. The cost is what the source took to run: media, commissions or content production, not salaries or overhead. That keeps sources comparable with each other, and explains why the figures are much shorter than the company-wide acquisition payback benchmarks, which count the whole sales and marketing budget. The formula stays visible and adjustable, for example to apply gross margin, so finance can check every figure.

Questions it answers

Which sources pay back, and how fast?

Each source shows what it cost next to the new annual recurring revenue its customers bring, credited according to the attribution model your team chose.

Payback translates directly into the return leadership asks for: a source that pays back in 4 months returns about three times its cost over the first year. What it doesn't show is how each cost behaves. Content costs what you decide to produce. Paid search prices move with competition and the season, so the same budget buys more or fewer clicks from one quarter to the next. A commission-based partner program costs nothing until a deal closes. Two sources with the same payback can carry very different risks.

Is a slow source a bad one?

Not necessarily. A multi-year contract signed with sales can take longer to cover its cost than a monthly plan, then keep paying for years. Whether 8 months is acceptable depends on how long those customers stay, which the same view shows by source.

Timing matters too. Organic search looks inexpensive over 12 months partly because it also earns from content published in earlier years, while paid search brings customers the day its budget is switched on and stops when it's switched off. One is cheaper per customer, the other can be scaled at will. Funnelsight reads each source on its own timeline, so a channel isn't cut for being slower to show results.

Why doesn't the attributed total match the CRM?

The CRM counts every deal closed in the period. Attribution can only credit a deal that has at least one touch your rules count.

What falls outside isn't an error, and its size depends on your rules. Some deals have no marketing touch at all, such as deals opened by outbound prospecting. Others have touches the rules leave out: older than the window, or recorded on someone the rules don't tie to the deal. A team that reads touches at account level credits activity from anyone at the account; one that counts only the contacts named on the opportunity credits less, often by choice, to keep opportunity creation light for sales.

A mistagged campaign is a different problem: it doesn't shrink the total, it credits the wrong source, often Direct. Funnelsight shows both totals side by side and explains what sits in the gap, with the CRM as the reference for revenue. Why marketing and sales pipeline numbers don't match goes further into the two readings.

How it works

Your team picks an attribution model, first touch, last touch, linear or position-based, and can adjust its weights, for example from 40-20-40 to 20-60-20. Putting two models side by side shows how much a ranking depends on the model rather than on the sources. Your team also sets what counts as a first touch and how far back touches are counted, often the length of your sales cycle.

Terms such as "sourced" or "influenced" vary from one company to another, so Funnelsight doesn't impose a definition: each reading your team names shows its rule. When a rule changes, the change is logged and past periods are recalculated or the break is marked, the principle behind a single source of truth.

What it does, and what it doesn't

It does

  • Recurring revenue and payback for each source, from the deals in your CRM
  • Costs from Google Ads, a spreadsheet or manual entry
  • Attribution models with visible, adjustable weights, two side by side
  • The CRM total next to the attributed figures, with the gap explained
  • Formulas finance can read and check

It doesn't

  • Use an algorithmic or "data-driven" attribution model
  • Model your marketing mix or forecast future payback: it reads past results
  • Prove that a touch caused a sale: it shows which touches came before it
  • Calculate sales compensation or pipeline coverage

Is payback a forecast?

No. Payback reads past results: the cost a source had over a period and the recurring revenue its customers brought. It shows how sources have performed, not how they will.

Can we include costs that aren't in Google Ads?

Yes. Costs from other ad platforms, events or content production can be imported from a spreadsheet or entered by hand. A source with no cost entered counts as zero, so it's worth checking which sources still have none.

Which attribution model should we start with?

The one that fits your sales cycle. A short cycle with one or two touches is easy to read with first or last touch, while a longer cycle with many touches is better served by a model that shares credit, such as position-based. Keep the same model when you compare periods.
