Marketing attribution and CAC payback by channel

Updated November 16, 2026

Paid search generates three times as many leads as partner referrals. Yet partners return more revenue for each dollar spent. Funnelsight follows every source through to the deals signed in your CRM, so you see the revenue each one brings and how quickly it earns back what it cost.

What is an attribution model, and which one should you use?

Before asking for a demo, a buyer may see a LinkedIn ad, read a blog article, join a webinar, then search for your name. Marketing attribution decides how much of the deal each of these touches gets. The rule you choose is called an attribution model.

Some models give all the credit to a single touch: the first one (first touch) or the last one before the request (last touch, also called last click). Multi-touch attribution shares it, equally between all touches (linear) or with more weight on the first and the last (U-shaped, also called position-based).

No model is right or wrong. First touch shows which channels bring in new buyers. Last touch shows which ones trigger the request. In B2B, where a deal takes several touches over weeks or months, U-shaped is a good default. It credits the channel that started the path and the one that closed it, without ignoring the rest.

Then compare two models side by side. In the table, the LinkedIn ad started the path but gets nothing under last touch. When a channel looks strong under one model and weak under the other, decide what you expect from it before you move its budget. And keep the same model from one period to the next, or a change of model will look like a change in results.

How to calculate CAC payback period by channel

CAC payback is the number of months a source takes to earn back what it cost.

Formula

CAC payback period (months) = cost of the source over the period ÷ new monthly recurring revenue from the customers it brought

Revenue comes from the deals in your CRM. Cost is what the source took to run: ad spend, partner commissions, content, tools. Salaries and overhead are left out, so sources can be compared with each other. That's why payback here is much shorter than company-wide benchmarks, which count the whole sales and marketing budget. Finance can check every figure, or adjust the formula, for example to use gross margin.

Two sources with the same payback can still carry different risks. Paid search prices rise and fall with competition and the season. A partner paid on commission costs nothing until a deal closes.

How to calculate marketing ROI by channel

ROI uses the same revenue and cost as payback. Payback tells you how long a channel takes to earn back its cost. ROI tells you how much it earned on top of that cost over a year.

Formula

Marketing ROI (%) = (new revenue from the channel − what the channel cost) ÷ what the channel cost × 100

Say a channel cost $100,000 over a year, and its customers signed $300,000 of yearly contracts. ROI = ($300,000 − $100,000) ÷ $100,000 = 200%. Each dollar spent came back as three. The same channel pays back in four months, since $300,000 a year is $25,000 a month.

Is a 200% ROI good?

Channel ROIs often look this high, because the calculation leaves a lot out. It counts the channel's own costs, not the team that runs it or the salespeople who close the deals. It counts revenue, not profit. And it stops after the first year. On this page, channels range from 100% to nearly 600%. Counting every salary and tool in sales and marketing, the same company's ROI is about 40%.

So compare your channels with each other, calculated the same way, rather than with another company's figure. A channel below 0% hasn't covered its cost in the first year. That can still be fine if its customers stay for several years.

Is a slow source a bad one?

Not necessarily. A multi-year contract signed by sales can take longer to cover its cost than a monthly plan, then keep paying for years. Whether 6 months is acceptable depends on how long those customers stay, which the same view shows by source.

Timing plays a part too. Organic search looks cheap over 12 months partly because it still earns from content published in earlier years. Paid search brings customers as soon as the budget is on, and stops when it's off. Funnelsight reads each source over its own timeline, so a channel isn't cut just because it's slower to show results.

Why doesn't the attributed total match the CRM?

The CRM counts every deal closed in the period. Attribution can only credit a deal that has at least one marketing touch your rules count.

The rest isn't an error. Some deals have no marketing touch at all, such as those opened by outbound prospecting. Others have touches your rules leave out, because they're older than the attribution window or come from a contact who isn't on the deal. A wrong or missing UTM parameter is a different case. The total stays the same, but the deal lands under the wrong source, usually Direct.

The question rarely comes from marketing. It tends to come up when someone compares marketing's figures with the CRM, for example in a pipeline review. Funnelsight shows both totals side by side and explains the gap, with the CRM as the reference for revenue.

How it works

Your team picks the attribution model, can change its weights, for example from 40-20-40 to 20-60-20, and can compare two models side by side. It also decides what counts as a touch, and how far back touches are counted (the attribution window). If you're not sure, start with the length of your average sales cycle.

Terms such as "sourced" or "influenced" mean different things from one company to another, so Funnelsight doesn't impose a definition. Each figure shows the rule behind it. When a rule changes, the change is logged, and past periods are recalculated or the break is marked. That's the principle behind a single source of truth.

Is payback a forecast?

No. Payback reads past results: the cost a source had over a period and the recurring revenue its customers brought. It shows how sources have performed, not how they will.

What's the difference between ROI and ROAS?

ROAS (return on ad spend) divides the revenue from your ads by what they cost: $300,000 for $100,000 of ads is a ROAS of 3. ROI looks at what you earned beyond the cost, and can include every cost of the channel, not only the ads. The same campaign has an ROI of 200%. In B2B, ad platforms usually see leads, not signed contracts. Use ROAS to compare ads, and ROI from your CRM to compare channels.

Can we include costs that no ad platform holds?

Yes. Partner commissions, events or content production can be imported from a spreadsheet or entered by hand. A source with no cost entered counts as zero, so it's worth checking which sources still have none.
