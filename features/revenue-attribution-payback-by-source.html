What each source is worth, and how fast it pays back

A source that brings the most leads isn't always the one that brings the most revenue. Funnelsight shows, for each source, the recurring revenue its customers generate and how many months that revenue takes to cover what the source cost.

Judge a source on what its customers bring

Lead volume is the easiest number to report, and the one most budget discussions start from. It says little about what happens next: some sources bring many sign-ups that never pay, others bring few that turn into long contracts. Looking at revenue and payback by source moves the conversation from which channel is loudest to which one is worth funding, and how long it needs before it can be judged.

How payback is calculated

Formula

Payback (months) = cost of the source over the period ÷ new monthly recurring revenue from the customers it brought

Revenue comes from the paying customers and deal amounts recorded in your CRM, including monthly plans, annual or multi-year contracts and renewals. Costs come from Google Ads when it's connected. Costs that no connected tool holds, such as other ad platforms, events or content production, can be imported from a spreadsheet or entered by hand. The cost here is what each source cost to run, such as media, commissions or content production, not salaries or overhead. That keeps sources comparable with each other, but makes the figures shorter than company-wide acquisition cost payback, which counts the whole sales and marketing budget. The formula is visible and adjustable, so a team that applies gross margin, or calculates acquisition cost per lead, gets the same figures it would calculate itself, and finance can check how each one is built.

Questions it answers

Which sources pay back, and how fast?

Each source shows its cost, the new recurring revenue its customers bring and the months it takes to cover that cost, credited according to the attribution model your team chose.

Payback also translates into the return leadership usually asks for: a source that pays back in 4 months returns about three times its cost over the first year. A slower payback isn't a verdict on its own, though. Whether 8 months is acceptable depends on how long those customers stay, which the same view shows by source. A source with no cost entered counts as zero, which is why Direct is flagged instead of topping the list.

Payback doesn't show how each cost behaves, and that matters when a budget has to be defended. Organic search costs what you choose to spend on content, so the budget is predictable. Paid search costs move with competition on your keywords and with the season, so the same budget can buy more or fewer clicks from one quarter to the next. In a commission-based partner program, you pay only when a deal closes: a partner campaign that brings nothing, or brings leads that never buy, costs nothing. Two sources with the same payback can carry very different risks.

Is a slow source a bad one?

Not necessarily. A monthly self-serve plan starts paying back from the first month, while a multi-year contract signed with sales can take longer to cover the cost of winning it and keep paying for years after. Timing works the same way: content published this quarter often brings customers months later, while paid search reacts within days. The same timing explains part of the gap in the card: organic search looks inexpensive over 12 months because it also earns from content published in earlier years, while paid search starts bringing customers the day the budget is switched on and stops when it's switched off. One is cheaper per customer, the other can be scaled up or down at will. Funnelsight reads each source on its own timeline, so a channel isn't cut for being slow when it is simply slower to show results.

Why doesn't the attributed total match the CRM?

Because the two answer different questions. The CRM counts every deal closed in the period. Attribution can only credit a deal to the touches it can see, inside the window your team set.

The gap isn't an error. It's made of deals the attribution has no touch to credit, contacts that were never linked to the deal and touches older than the window. Dates add a further shift: attribution follows the date of each touch, while sales reports follow the date a deal was created or closed. Funnelsight shows both totals side by side and explains the difference, with the CRM as the reference for pipeline and revenue. Why marketing and sales pipeline numbers don't match goes further into how the two readings differ.

How it works

Your team picks an attribution model: first touch, last touch, linear or position-based, with weights you can adjust, for example from 40-20-40 to 20-60-20. Two models can sit side by side, which shows how much a ranking depends on the model rather than on the sources. Your team also decides what counts as a first touch and how far back touches are counted, often based on the length of your sales cycle.

Terms such as "sourced" or "influenced" mean different things from one company to another, so Funnelsight doesn't impose a definition. Each reading your team names shows its rule: which touches count, over what window, and how credit is split. When a rule changes, the change is logged and past periods are recalculated or the break is clearly marked, the same principle described in what a single source of truth means in SaaS.

What it does, and what it doesn't

It does

  • Recurring revenue and payback for each source, from the deals in your CRM
  • Costs from Google Ads, a spreadsheet or manual entry
  • Attribution models with visible, adjustable weights, two side by side
  • The CRM total next to the attributed figures, with the gap explained
  • Formulas finance can read and check

It doesn't

  • Use an algorithmic or "data-driven" attribution model
  • Model your marketing mix or forecast future payback: it reads past results
  • Prove that a touch caused a sale: it shows which touches came before it
  • Calculate sales compensation or pipeline coverage

Is payback a forecast?

No. Payback reads past results: the cost a source had over a period and the recurring revenue its customers brought. It shows how sources have performed, not how they will.

Can we include costs that aren't in Google Ads?

Yes. Costs from other ad platforms, events or content production can be imported from a spreadsheet or entered by hand. A source with no cost entered counts as zero, so it's worth checking which sources still have none.

Which attribution model should we start with?

The one that fits your sales cycle and what you want to learn. A short cycle with one or two touches is easy to read with first or last touch, while a longer cycle with many touches is better served by a model that shares credit, such as position-based. Keep the same model when you compare periods, and put a second one beside it to see how much the ranking depends on the choice.
